How this page works

This page lists new research papers for one reader, Jake Bowers, who works on research design, causal inference, randomization-based inference, policy evaluation, and political methodology. Every evening at 7:30 pm Central time, a Mac mini in Jake's house starts a program that rebuilds the page. The time is half an hour after arXiv announces its new papers, which it does at 7:00 pm Central from Sunday through Thursday.

The program first gathers papers. From arXiv it takes the papers announced that evening. From the other sources listed below it takes the papers they added in the past seven days. For each paper it keeps the title, the authors, and the abstract. It then drops every paper it ranked on an earlier evening.

Next the program puts the remaining papers in order. Jake gave it 62 passages of his own to compare each new paper with: the abstracts of 48 of his papers, and 14 paragraphs he wrote about what he wants to read. Each paper he votes up, as described under "Votes" below, becomes one more passage. A small language model turns each text, whether a new paper's title and abstract or one of Jake's passages, into a list of numbers. The model's makers trained it so that two texts on the same subject get similar lists. For each new paper, the program finds the one passage of Jake's whose list is most like the paper's. The paper whose list comes closest to its passage's list goes first. The section "How the papers are put in order" below says how the program measures how close two lists are.

Last, the program adds the first 20 papers in that order to the page, keeps the papers it added on the six evenings before, and sends the new page to GitHub, which publishes it at papers.jakebowers.org.

Drew Dimmery runs a page of the same kind, https://papers.ddimmery.com/. His program also turns abstracts into lists of numbers to rank new papers, and then has a larger language model choose a few and write one sentence on each. His code is not public, so this program is new code. This program has no step in which a language model writes a sentence.

Where the papers come from

The program reads the sources below. From arXiv it takes the papers announced that evening. From each of the others it takes every paper added in the past seven days.

  • arXiv, in five categories: methodology (stat.ME), machine learning (stat.ML), applied statistics (stat.AP), econometrics (econ.EM), and computers and society (cs.CY).
  • SocArXiv, the social science preprint server.
  • NBER working papers.
  • 24 journals in statistics, political science, sociology, biometrics, and econometrics, among them the Journal of the American Statistical Association, Biometrika, Political Analysis, and the American Political Science Review.
  • Any journal at all, for papers that OpenAlex, a free index of research papers, files under its topic "Advanced Causal Inference Techniques".

Crossref, the agency that registers DOIs, the permanent identifiers of papers, says which papers the journals and NBER published each day. It usually lacks the abstract, so the program asks OpenAlex for it. When neither has one, the page shows the paper with no abstract, and the program ranks it by its title alone.

The program asks the sources other than arXiv for a whole week every evening, so that a paper one of them was slow to list is found the next day. arXiv's evening announcement lists only that evening's papers, so on an evening when the program does not run, it misses that evening's arXiv papers. The program remembers every paper it has already ranked and ranks each one only once. A new arXiv version, or the journal version of a preprint, counts as a new paper. Papers not in English are left out.

How the papers are put in order

Jake gave the program two kinds of text to compare new papers with: the abstracts of 48 of his own papers, and 14 paragraphs he wrote about what he wants to read. Call each of these 62 texts a reference passage.

The program compares texts with a small language model, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, which runs on the computer in Jake's house. The model reads a passage and returns a list of 1,024 numbers. A list of 1,024 numbers can be drawn as an arrow in 1,024 dimensions, just as a list of two numbers is an arrow on a flat page. Its makers trained it so that passages on similar subjects get arrows that point in nearly the same direction. So the angle between two arrows measures how close two passages are in subject. Two passages that share no subject at all have arrows at about 90 degrees.

For each new paper, the program computes the angle between the arrow of the paper's title and abstract and the arrow of each of the 62 reference passages. The paper's score is the smallest of those 62 angles, and the page names the reference passage that gave it, on the line "Closest to". The bar beside that line restates the angle: it is empty at 90 degrees and full at 30 degrees or less. The papers picked so far fall between about 40 and 60 degrees.

Each evening the page adds up to 20 of the day's new papers, smallest angle first, and shows the past seven days.

Votes

Under each paper are two links, "more like this" and "fewer like this". Each opens a GitHub form, and submitting the form files a vote in the program's private GitHub repository. Only people with access to that repository can file one, so only Jake can vote. A paper Jake votes up becomes one more reference passage. When a new paper's closest passage is a paper he voted down, the program puts the new paper below every other paper of that day. A vote counts for one year.

Earlier papers

The row at the top of the page names the past seven days. The page opens on the newest day that has papers, and a tap on another day shows that day's papers instead. "All days" shows all seven. "Month" opens a calendar with the number of papers the program picked each day. A day on the calendar opens that day's papers, and "Week" opens all the papers of one week. The papers by followed authors and the Semantic Scholar recommendations, described below, leave the page after seven days and do not appear on the calendar.

Followed authors

Jake keeps a list of people he follows. Each evening the program asks OpenAlex, an index of research papers, for every paper by those people published in the past seven days. OpenAlex knows each person by an identifier, because a search by name alone would also find everyone else with that name, so the list gives each person's identifiers. The page shows those papers first, under the heading "From authors Jake follows," whether or not their abstracts come close to Jake's work. A paper can appear there and also among a day's picks.

A paper with more than four authors shows the first three, then any author on Jake's list, then the number of other authors. Following an author does not change a paper's place in the day's list.

Semantic Scholar recommendations

Each evening the program also sends 18 of Jake's methods papers to Semantic Scholar, a search service for research papers, as examples. Semantic Scholar answers with up to 20 papers it finds similar, from everything it indexes, so they can come from places the program does not read. Most are recent. The program leaves out any paper it has already picked or shown as a recommendation, adds the first 5 of the rest to the page under the heading "Semantic Scholar recommends," below the day's papers, and keeps each there for seven days. Semantic Scholar, not the program, chose these papers and their order. Jake can vote on them as he does on the day's papers.

What the page does not do

The page does not judge whether a paper is correct or good. The program measures only how close a paper's words come to Jake's, and it reads only the sources listed above, apart from the papers by followed authors and the Semantic Scholar recommendations. A paper whose abstract uses words unlike Jake's gets a large angle and a low place, however relevant its mathematics is.
