ceTDP: A Practical Tutorial on Valid and Quantitative Cluster-Extent Inference in Neuroimaging

Xu Chen, Lucas Peek, Ramin Monajemi, Jelle J. Goeman, and 3 others · bioRxiv (Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory) · posted 25 November 2026

We introduce ceTDP, a two-stage framework for cluster-extent inference in neuroimaging that extends conventional random field theory-based and permutation-based methods. In contrast to standard cluster inference, which yields binary significance, ceTDP additionally quantifies the proportion of truly active voxels within clusters using the true discovery proportion (TDP). The method is built on closed testing and provides strong family-wise error rate control while remaining consistent with standard cluster inference. The framework operates in two stages: estimation of a cluster-extent threshold, followed by computation of the TDP lower bound. Two complementary procedures are provided: a conservative, statistically guaranteed TDP lower bound (ceTDP-cons) and a heuristic approach for obtaining a potentially tighter estimate of the TDP lower bound (ceTDP-heur). Together, they enable both rigorous inference and improved sensitivity in cluster-level analysis.

Combining Dependent p-Values: Methods and Properties from a Practical Perspective

Matteo Gasparin, Jelle J. Goeman, Ruth Heller, Aldo Solari · Annual Review of Statistics and Its Application · published 24 November 2026

Testing the global null hypothesis, that all of a collection of m null hypotheses are true, is a central task in statistical inference. This article reviews recent and established tests that approach this task by combining the m hypotheses’ p -values, focusing on the situation that these p -values are dependent and that the precise form of this dependence is unknown. We provide guidelines for choosing p -value combination functions based on theoretical results and numerical experiments. Bonferroni and Simes represent the classical approaches; the former offers uniform validity against any dependence structure, while the latter is robust to many practical dependencies and uniformly more powerful. We also discuss the merits and promises of novel approaches, such as the Cauchy and harmonic mean p -value combination functions, twice-the-median, and the recent uniform improvement of Hommel's combination function. The permutation approach is recommended whenever the null distribution is invariant to permutations of the data. Finally, we emphasize that global testing is often just a single but important step in a bigger inferential framework and illustrate the utility of such tests within modern statistical practice.

Causal Artificial Intelligence for Multidimensional Poverty Reduction and Adaptive Social Protection

Murali Krishna Pasupuleti · International Journal of Academic and Industrial Research Innovations(IJAIRI) · published 23 November 2026

A social protection agency allocating emergency transfers faces two questions. Which households face the greatest deprivation, and whose deprivation can a timely additional payment reduce? Answering the second requires evidence on outcomes under alternative offers of assistance. This paper develops a causal allocation framework for discretionary top-ups that protects existing entitlements and makes the trade-off between reach and expected benefit explicit. It combines a weighted deprivation outcome, a design for estimating the effect of an offer, and a budget-constrained allocation rule with minimum coverage requirements. A secondary analysis reconstructs adjusted poverty headcounts from published incidence and intensity estimates for programme participants and non-participants in Ethiopia, India and Peru across four survey waves. They also expose the limits of using programme participation histories to predict individual treatment gains. The paper specifies a prospective cluster-randomised evaluation with independent outcome measurement, a fixed-budget comparator and explicit tests for differential exclusion. The contribution is a testable link between the poverty measure an agency adopts, the intervention effect it can identify and the allocation decision its budget permits. Administrative Deserts

Sebastian Jilke · socarxiv · posted 27 November 2026

For most people, the state is not an institution encountered in the abstract. Rather, it is a specific office, a particular counter, and a frontline worker who may or may not help them accomplish what they came for. These routine citizen-state interactions such as renewing a license, obtaining identification, registering a vehicle, or accessing benefits, constitute the primary means through which people experience state capacity – the government’s ability to effectively implement policy and deliver services. Yet, we lack systematic understanding of why these experiences often vary by frontline office. This article introduces the concept of administrative deserts, which are areas where government administrative services systematically underperform despite the physical presence of offices and frontline workers. Synthesizing the literature on state capacity, street-level bureaucracy, and spatial inequality, we develop a framework explaining administrative deserts as variation in citizen-experienced state capacity across offices. We specify five scope conditions enabling desert formation and identify feedback dynamics through which deserts persist. We illustrate the framework using descriptive evidence from Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) offices across the State of New Jersey, revealing substantial variation in citizen-experienced state capacity that correlates with neighborhood disadvantage. The framework advances public administration theory by showing how the same bureaucracy, operating under identical policies, can deliver systematically different state capacity depending on where people live, thereby creating a hidden geography of democratic citizenship where experienced state capacity does not depend on policy, but place.

What Does the Kernel Estimator Recover When Treatment Effects Are Nonlinear? A Comment on Hainmueller, Mummolo and Xu (2019)

Josep Serrano-Serrat · socarxiv · posted 27 November 2026

Simonsohn (2024a,b) argues that when treatment effects are nonlinear, the kernel estimator of Hainmueller, Mummolo and Xu (2019) finds spurious interactions if the treatment and the moderator are correlated. In response, Hainmueller et al. (2025) make two related points: (i) researchers should first define their estimand, with the conditional marginal effect (CME) being a natural one for studying effect heterogeneity; and (ii) the kernel estimator recovers the CME as the sample grows. Taking their important call to define the estimand as a starting point, this comment shows that their second result holds only under specific conditions. As the authors show, for any given value of the moderator, in large samples the kernel estimator converges to the slope of a linear regression of the outcome on the treatment. This is the conditional best linear predictor (CBLP), which weights the effect of the treatment at each dose differently than the CME. Under an arbitrary function linking the treatment and the outcome, the two coincide only when the treatment is normally distributed at all values of the moderator, which is the case analyzed by Hainmueller et al. (2025). Using the same data-generating process with a treatment that is not normally distributed, I show that the kernel estimates track the CBLP and diverge from the CME. Alternatives, such as GAMs, may be used to descriptively to assess the severity of the problem associated with nonlinear treatment effects. The comment concludes by discussing some implications of considering CBLP rather than CME as the quantity of interest.

Our findings suggest that issue polarization changes which relational channels citizens draw on, increasing the importance of political agreement and network embeddedness, while reducing the role of routine contact. Methodologically, the results show that reliance on “general political discussion” survey items masks important variation in tie activation processes across topics.

This work presents a stylized computable general equilibrium (CGE) model for analysing circular economy policy strategies in an intentionally abstract setting. The model is not calibrated to a specific economy; instead, it uses round-number calibration data and systematic numerical experiments to identify the behavioural and physical conditions under which circular strategies reduce primary material use, represented by virgin metal. The analysis compares virgin-metal taxation, recycling support, and life-extension support through refurbishment, repair, and reuse in single-country and two-country model variants. The results show that circular strategies cannot be ranked by label alone. Support-efficiency comparisons show that refurbishment support performs better than recycling support in the closed-economy case, while this gap narrows when virgin-metal production is separated from consumption and circular processing in the two-country extension. The nested life-extension analysis further shows that refurbishment, repair, and reuse are not interchangeable policy mechanisms. Overall, the model provides a reproducible equilibrium framework for identifying where circular economy strategies work, where they fail, and which assumptions drive the difference.

How the Experience of Administrative Burdens Affects Clients’ Psychological Well-being: The Role of Negativity Bias

Jaeyeong Nam, Sebastian Jilke, Pamela Herd, Donald Moynihan · socarxiv · posted 27 November 2026

A core claim of the administrative burden literature is that the experience of frictions during administrative encounters can generate psychological costs. Using a recall experiment, we show that being asked to recall administrative encounters in a welfare program negatively affects people's psychological affect. Further, we show that the responses to burdens are asymmetric and consistent with a pattern of negativity bias in how people recall citizen-state encounters. Being asked to recall encounters induces negative affect, even though the majority of respondents reported having experienced low levels of administrative burden. Respondents' negative psychological reactions are driven by individuals who rated their experience as burdensome in survey questions or reported a bad experience in open-ended responses. Positive reported experiences were not associated with more positive affect. The results also suggest that the relationship between the experience of burden and negative affect is more pronounced for women, liberals, Whites, and those in poor or fair health. Moreover, those with neutral political ideology and those in very good or excellent health experienced decreased positive affect. A key theoretical implication is that the experience of psychological costs is shaped by negativity bias: negative interactions have a larger effect on outcomes than positive interactions. A practical implication is that governments should focus attention on reducing burdensome or otherwise negative encounters.

Design-based causal inference for ROC curves

Bufan Li, Haoyang Yu · arxiv · posted 26 November 2026 · stat.ME

In randomized experiments, causal inference traditionally focuses on the average treatment effect, yet comparing the treatment and control potential outcome distributions is often of interest. Under complete randomization, we adopt a design-based framework without imposing an outcome model to study the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, which measures the proportion of treated outcomes exceeding each upper quantile of the control distribution. We propose an estimator that depends only on pooled outcome ranks, establish its asymptotic normality, and construct conservative variance estimators and confidence intervals. We also develop covariate adjustment directly for the ROC estimator, preserving the target estimand while achieving an asymptotic variance no larger than that of the unadjusted estimator. We further construct simultaneous confidence bands over an interior range of quantile levels and extend pointwise inference to the extreme upper tail of the control distribution, with both extensions accommodating covariate adjustment. Simulation studies and an application to a large-scale online learning experiment illustrate the proposed methods.

The Myth of the Random Sample

M. Borgstede, F. Eggert · Psychological Test Adaptation and Development · published 1 October 2026

Abstract: Statistical significance tests are generally understood as tools to test hypotheses about population effects based on random samples. However, many psychological experiments use nonrandom samples. We argue that the general logic of null-hypothesis testing does not depend on random sampling, but drawing inferences about population effects does. Significance tests only require that the test statistic is a random variable with a known distribution under the null hypothesis. In parametric tests, this distribution is analytically derived from assumptions about the population. However, the distribution of any test statistic can also be determined directly from the data, using random permutations (or re-randomizations), the only assumption being that the data are exchangeable under the null hypothesis. Ironically, exchangeability always holds if the data come from a randomized experiment. Therefore, the use of significance tests is valid for inferences about the treatment effects in randomized experiments, but their interpretation as tools of statistical generalization is not.
