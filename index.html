Network Effect Decomposition for Causal Effect Estimation under Network Interference

Zhirong Huang, Wenzhen Zhang, Guangquan Lu, and 3 others · arxiv

Abstract

Estimating causal effects from observational network data requires accounting for both individual treatment and neighborhood exposure. Existing outcome models commonly incorporate these intervention variables into a joint predictor and estimate causal effects through prediction contrasts, leaving the corresponding response components implicit. We propose the $\underline{N}$etwork $\underline{E}$ffect $\underline{D}$ecomposition $\underline{Net}$work (NEDNet), which parameterizes the conditional outcome response as a query-invariant pre-treatment baseline plus separate treatment- and exposure-response pathways. Path-specific neighborhood contextualization supplies local pre-treatment information to the two pathways. We characterize the fitted response through a unique reference-normalized decomposition, which is equivalent to a zero mixed treatment--exposure contrast. Experiments on semi-synthetic benchmarks derived from BlogCatalog and Flickr show that NEDNet achieves the lowest PEHE among the compared methods for individual, peer, and total effects across homogeneous and covariate-heterogeneous response settings.

The Illusion of Learning from Observational Data: An Empirical Bayes Perspective

Bohan Wu, Sebastian Salazar, Donald P. Green, David M. Blei · arxiv

Abstract

Randomized experiments have long been the gold standard for scientists seeking to learn about cause and effect. When randomized experiments are infeasible, scientists often resort to observational studies, which are widely available and often large but rely on untestable assumptions that, when violated, may result in biased estimates. Uncertainty about bias leads to a phenomenon known as the illusion of learning from observational research (Gerber, Green and Kaplan, 2004a): absent prior information about bias, observational results cannot meaningfully contribute to the estimation of a causal parameter. To shatter the illusion, we take an empirical Bayes perspective. We show that the distribution of observational biases can be learned from calibration studies-experiments that target a causal effect that is known a priori to be zero. Calibration identifies the distribution of observational bias and allows observational studies to inform the estimation of causal parameters via empirical Bayes shrinkage. We formalize the illusion phenomenon in an empirical Bayes setting and show that, with an increasing number of calibration and observation studies, both the bias distribution and the causal effect can be consistently recovered. We illustrate our method through a simulation study and a semi-synthetic application based on Ferraro and Miranda (2013)'s water-usage experiment.

Regression Adjusted Target Effects Using Auxiliary Strata Under Design‐Based Inference

Zixuan Zhao, Yuehan Yang · Statistics in Medicine

Abstract

We propose a design-based covariate adjustment that leverages information from auxiliary strata to improve the efficiency of average treatment effect estimation when target samples are sparse. The estimator borrows strength from related strata that may be correlated with the target, yielding gains for studies with limited observations or rare subpopulations. We present the estimator, develop a Neyman-style conservative variance estimator for hypothesis testing and confidence interval construction, and establish its large-sample properties. The procedure is straightforward to implement within standard randomization-based workflows and does not require a correctly specified outcome model or stochastic error assumptions. In simulations, the method delivers lower variance and narrower confidence intervals than conventional design-based or regression-adjusted estimators across a range of sparsity and correlation patterns. Applications to a schooling experiment and the ACTG 175 randomized trial illustrate practical gains in precision.

When Statisticians Can Also Be AI Engineers, What Could We Build?

Ruoqing Zhu · arxiv

Abstract

Statistical software makes methods widely available. Good statistical work, however, requires understanding the question, how the data were collected, and what conclusions the evidence can support. As AI agents make data analysis accessible to people without statistical training, this reasoning should be built into the systems themselves. Our review of selected AI agents for data analysis and study design points to a gap in documented support for scientific inquiry with users at runtime. In this paper, we advocate designing domain-specific interactive harnesses in which statistical principles guide proactive inquiry: agents recognize missing information that could change an analysis, ask questions users may not know to raise, and use the answers to guide or revise analytical decisions. Two practical entry points illustrate how statisticians can begin building: an implemented causal-consulting skill within an existing agent environment and Model Forge, a custom harness for interactive model and algorithm development. These design examples connect statistical principles to choices about context, memory, tools, and interfaces, and motivate evaluation of both computational correctness and the scientific consequences of interaction. We conclude with a broader interdisciplinary research agenda on adaptive human-agent interaction, including selective information acquisition, evolving user preferences, and evaluation under uncertainty.

Identification and Bounding of Joint Expectation over Potential Outcomes

Yuta Kawakami, Jin Tian · arxiv

Abstract

The joint expectation of functions of potential outcomes $(Y_1,Y_0)$ is a fundamental quantity in causal inference, particularly when investigating the variation or heterogeneity of causal effects. This paper provides novel assumptions for their identification: comonotonicity and countercomonotonicity. These assumptions offer a unified framework for their identification across discrete, continuous, and mixed outcome variables. In the absence of these assumptions, we derive sharp bounds for two broad classes of functions, yielding new results for bounding moments of individual causal effects, which are central to measuring effect heterogeneity. We present corresponding estimation methods and illustrate them in simulation studies and real-world datasets.

Inference after selection on randomized p-values

Ronan Perry, Daniela Witten · arxiv

Abstract

We consider inference on parameters and hypotheses selected using p-values. We provide a simple procedure to decompose a p-value into two components, one for inference and one for selection, that are independent under the null hypothesis. To control the Type 1 error rate conditional on selection of the null hypothesis from randomized p-values, we develop tests motivated by the selective inference approaches of data thinning, fission, and carving. Unlike the typical application of these approaches, however, our tests only make use of the p-values rather than the full data. Furthermore, we construct confidence intervals that attain the nominal conditional coverage and corresponding estimators. In the context of the file drawer problem, the winner's curse, and sequential testing, we show that our proposals offer both theoretical and empirical advantages over existing randomized and non-randomized approaches. Lastly, we apply our methods for selective inference on genetic associations using publicly-available p-values from the UK Biobank.

Langevin-gradient rerandomization

Antonio Carlos Herling Ribeiro · Statistics and Computing

Abstract

Abstract Rerandomization is an experimental design technique that repeatedly randomizes treatment assignments until covariates are balanced between treatment groups. Rerandomization in the design stage of an experiment can lead to many asymptotic benefits in the analysis stage, such as increased precision, increased statistical power for hypothesis testing, reduced sensitivity to model specification, and mitigation of p-hacking. However, the standard implementation of rerandomization via rejection sampling faces a severe computational bottleneck in high-dimensional settings, where the probability of finding an acceptable randomization vanishes. Although alternatives based on Metropolis-Hastings and constrained optimization techniques have been proposed, these alternatives rely on discrete procedures that lack information from the gradient of the covariate balance metric, limiting their efficiency in high-dimensional spaces. We propose Langevin-Gradient Rerandomization (LGR), a new sampling method that mitigates this dimensionality challenge by navigating a continuous relaxation of the treatment assignment space with a Langevin diffusion. We discuss the trade-offs of this approach, specifically that LGR samples from a non-uniform distribution over the set of balanced randomizations. We demonstrate how to retain valid inference under this design using randomization tests and empirically show that LGR generates acceptable randomizations orders of magnitude faster than current rerandomization methods in high dimensions.

Estimands for Randomized Discontinuation Designs in Oncology

Ayon Mukherjee, Oleksandr Sverdlov, Yu Deng · Pharmaceutical Statistics

Abstract

The Randomized Discontinuation Design (RDD) is a two-stage adaptive enrichment design that is uniquely suited to answer causal questions about the benefit of continuing treatment in patients who have already demonstrated response to an induction therapy. The estimand framework from the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) E9(R1) addendum provides the necessary language to precisely define this scientific question. A major challenge in formulating estimands for RDD oncology trials is that the target population for the primary scientific question is itself defined by a post-treatment biological outcome in Stage 1, which makes it difficult to separate the process of selecting eligible participants from the causal inference problem in Stage 2. In this paper, we address this challenge using the Phase III JAVELIN Gastric 100 trial as an example. We argue that the "conventional" estimand can be formulated as an estimand targeting the response-defined subgroup of participants with evidence of response to the induction therapy in Stage 1, and we clarify the precise sense in which this subgroup is, and is not, related to the concept of a principal stratum. We also formulate a "full-path" estimand-capturing the patients' entire two-stage treatment journey-and connect it to the dynamic treatment regime literature. We also provide Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) for the two estimands to clarify their respective causal structures. This paper aims to strengthen the dialogue among clinical research stakeholders tasked with the design and analysis of RDD trials and support more efficient regulatory decision-making. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02625610.

Beyond Good and Bad Controls: Judicial Prejudice and Differential Measurement Error in Sentencing Research

Jose Pina-Sánchez, Andrzej Uhl · socarxiv

Abstract

Observational studies of institutional decision-making often rely on explanatory variables that are themselves partly constructed through the process under investigation. In sentencing research, many legal factors are defined by the judge and may therefore act simultaneously as confounders and mediators. Controlling for them may introduce post-treatment bias, whereas omitting them may introduce confounding bias. We propose overcoming this impasse by treating the influence of judicial prejudice on these legal factors as a form of differential measurement error. Using a typical regression model of racial disparities in the U.S. Federal Courts, we demonstrate how Monte Carlo simulations can be used to examine changes in estimates of racial disparity under different assumptions about the extent to which judicial prejudice is present in the construction of offence seriousness and pre-trial detention. We show that minor differential errors in some of these legal factors can substantially mask estimates of racial disparities in sentence length, and why the convention of reporting models with and without discretionary factors fails to reflect this form of model uncertainty.

Companion AI and Psychological Health: Resource or Risk? Affective Design as the Mechanism of the Human–Machine Bond

Oliver Alexandre Reinis · socarxiv

Abstract

This article examines, through a critical and integrative review of the literature (2024–2026), the extent to which affective bonds with companion artificial intelligences constitute a resource or a risk for psychological health, considering attachment, loneliness, well-being, and identity, and how the affective design of these systems produces such effects. The analysis indicates that the human–machine bond is a predictable effect of an affective engineering that simulates reciprocity and continuity (intimacy by design; designed relationality), rather than an error or deficit on the part of the user. Its effects, however, are neither intrinsically beneficial nor harmful: they act as an amplifier of the user’s prior psychological trajectory (AI Amplifier Effect), modulated by self-regulation, peer community, and sociocultural context. It is concluded that the bond is real yet asymmetric and functional, and that clinical and ethical evaluation should shift from the question of whether AI replaces the human bond to the question of what kind of bond affective design produces, in whom, and under what conditions—regulating the design rather than pathologizing the user.

The Male Abstraction Bias in Internet Culture and Large Language Models

Douglas Guilbeault, Bhargav Srinivasa Desikan · socarxiv

Abstract

Conceptual abstraction and gender are two equally central aspects of the human mind, yet they have traditionally been studied in isolation from one another, as though they were fundamentally separate. Here, we find gender and abstraction are systematically linked throughout the space of concepts spanning single (N = 40,000) and multi-word expressions (N = 67,000): across billions of words and millions of images on the internet --- and across numerous measurement techniques, including human and AI classifications --- more abstract concepts are more associated with men, a pattern we show replicates in languages around the world. This male abstraction bias not only holds across semantic domains, but it also maps onto grammatical structure: verbs and adverbs are more abstract and more male-coded, revealing a system-wide trace of stereotypes accentuating agentic qualities in men. Furthermore, we show this bias readily emerges in the free associations humans and AIs produce in controlled online tasks, indicating its psychological internalization and its algorithmic reproduction. These findings identify a latent structure in conceptual space that unifies a range of stereotypes: for example, framings of women as more embodied, emotional, and warm, versus men as more cognitive, rational, and competent; as well as erroneous views of men as better at mathematics, science, and engineering. More broadly, by analyzing an unprecedently large, multimodal sample of online data, we provide culture-wide evidence of gender as a core organizing principle shaping conceptual space far beyond the characterization of people and social roles.

Sophia Fauser, Emanuela Struffolino · socarxiv

Abstract

Objective This brief report examines German parents’ post-birth employment trajectories, highlighting gender differences in mismatches between work-hour preferences and actual employment. Background Previous studies on post-birth employment rarely considered work-hour preferences, while research on such preferences rarely takes a longitudinal perspective. We bridge these two literature streams by using a dynamic approach, illustrating how actual-to-preferred-hour mismatches evolve when the youngest child is aged 0-3. Method We apply multichannel sequence and fuzzy clustering analysis on data from the German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP, 2008-2023), restricting our sample to 645 cohabitating couples. Next, we use Dirichlet regression to link the identified (fuzzy) patterns to individual, household, and contextual characteristics. Results Among mothers, mismatches between actual and preferred working hours increase as children grow older, with many mothers employed in short part-time jobs wishing to work more hours (i.e., underemployment), while most full-time working mothers would prefer to reduce their hours (i.e., overemployment). Dirichlet regression reveals that among fathers, especially job characteristics are associated with overemployment, while individual characteristics and constraints are associated with longer-term preferences for inactivity among mothers. Conclusion Results highlight that overemployment and employment matched with preferences is much more common among parents than underemployment, leaving little room for reallocation of paid and unpaid work within couples.

A Simple Sensitivity Analysis for Observational Studies

Jeffrey Zhang, Dylan S. Small · CHANCE

Abstract

Drawing causal conclusions from non-experimental (observational) data is a central aim in science but is extremely challenging. Without control over treatment assignment (as in an experiment), treated and control groups may not be comparable on unmeasured factors affecting treatment and outcome. To address such concerns, sensitivity analyses can be performed; sensitivity analyses acknowledge the existence of unmeasured factors but ask how strong they must be to overturn causal conclusions. We (re)introduce a particularly simple form of sensitivity analysis which can be performed by any reader of an observational study with access to only an estimate and confidence interval for a causal effect. This simple sensitivity analysis may serve as an effective tool for readers wishing to gauge the robustness of conclusions from an observational study.

Does Rerandomization Help Beyond Covariate Adjustment? A Review and Guide for Theory and Practice

Ant\^onio Carlos Herling Ribeiro Junior, Zach Branson · arxiv

Abstract

Rerandomization is a modern experimental design technique that repeatedly randomizes treatment assignments until covariates are deemed balanced between treatment groups. This enhances the precision and coherence of causal effect estimators, mitigates false discoveries from p-hacking, and increases statistical power. Recent work suggests that balancing covariates via rerandomization does not alter the asymptotic precision of covariate-adjusted estimators, thereby making it unclear whether rerandomization is worthwhile if adjusted estimators are used. However, these results have two key caveats. First, these results are asymptotic, leaving finite sample performance unknown. Second, these results focus on precision, while other potential benefits, such as increased coherence among flexible estimators, remain understudied. Hence, in this paper we provide three main contributions: (i) a comprehensive review of the rerandomization literature, covering historical foundations, theoretical developments, and recent methodological advancements, (ii) an extensive simulation study examining finite-sample performance, and (iii) a practical guide for practitioners. Our study compares precision, coherence, power, and coverage of various estimators under rerandomization versus complete randomization. We find rerandomization to be a complementary design strategy that enhances the precision, robustness, and reliability of causal effect estimators, especially for smaller sample sizes.

Finite-Sample FDR Control for Greedy Aggregation over Networks

Mehrdad Pournaderi · arxiv

Abstract

Distributed multiple testing asks $N$ sites, each holding p-values for its own hypotheses, to control the false discovery rate (FDR) of the discoveries made across the whole network while communicating only a small number of bits. Greedy interval aggregation (Pournaderi and Xiang, IEEE TSIPN, 2023) meets the communication budget but controls FDR only asymptotically, and its FDR can exceed the target at finite sample sizes; the same interval counts both select the rejection regions and calibrate the stopping rule, which biases the selected interval densities upward. We propose \emph{budgeted BONuS-GA}: each site mixes synthetic uniform p-values into its data, the center ranks candidate p-value intervals across sites using the pooled counts, and each site's false discoveries are estimated from its own synthetic counts under a per-site share of the level. The procedure needs no knowledge of the null proportions, uses every p-value for both selection and inference, and controls $FDR\le\alpha$ at every sample size for any fixed assignment of hypotheses to sites, assuming only that the null p-values are independent uniforms, independent of the non-null p-values. It keeps the original $O(\sqrt m\log m)$ communication, $m$ being the total number of p-values, when $N=O(\sqrt m)$. We also give a sample-splitting alternative, cross-fit greedy aggregation, with finite-sample control under a random-site model, and we quantify the selection bias behind the original procedure's failure. In a monitoring-network simulation the budgeted procedure retains most of the power of its heuristic counterpart at moderate-to-large site sizes.

Efficient and Globally Robust Causal Excursion Effect Estimation

Zhaoxi Cheng, Lauren Bell, Tianchen Qian · arxiv

Abstract

Causal excursion effect (CEE) characterizes the effect of an intervention under policies that deviate from the experimental policy. It is widely used to study the effect of time-varying interventions that have the potential to be frequently adaptive, such as those delivered through smartphones. We study the semiparametric efficient estimation of CEE and we derive a semiparametric efficiency bound for CEE under working assumptions, in the context of micro-randomized trials. We propose a class of two-stage estimators that achieve the efficiency bound and are robust to misspecified nuisance models. In deriving the asymptotic property of the estimators, we establish a collection of easy-to-use theorems for globally robust Z-estimators with either cross-fitted or non-cross-fitted nuisance parameters that are applicable beyond the proposed estimators for CEE. We demonstrate efficiency gain of the proposed estimator compared to existing ones through simulations and a real data application using the Drink Less micro-randomized trial.

ThSQCA: An R Package for Threshold-Sweep Qualitative Comparative Analysis

Yuki Toyoda · socarxiv

Abstract

Qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) requires thresholds to construct sets from continuous scales, but a standard analysis is fixed to a single set of thresholds and does not directly reveal how the sufficiency structure changes when the target level of the outcome or the judgment criterion for a condition changes. This article introduces the R package ThSQCA, which uses the QCA package as its computational engine and provides a common workflow to run, aggregate, visualize, and report analyses that systematically vary the outcome threshold, the condition thresholds, or both. Using simulated data constructed for methodological illustration, we show that raising the target level of the outcome narrows the result from multiple sufficient paths to a single path (with lower consistency and higher coverage) and eventually yields no solution. We also show that varying the judgment criterion for a condition can make a solution disappear or produce a solution supported by very few cases. ThSQCA does not select an appropriate threshold automatically; it is a diagnostic workflow that clarifies the range over which a conclusion holds and the points at which it changes.

A Simulation and Real-Data Evaluation of Causal Discovery Algorithms under Realistic Social Science Conditions

Sacha Varin · Asian Journal of Probability and Statistics

Abstract

Background: Causal discovery algorithms aim to infer causal structure from observational data under explicit assumptions, yet their performance under realistic social science conditions remains insufficiently understood. Existing evaluations typically rely on large samples, high-dimensional settings, and clean data—conditions that rarely hold in applied research. Objectives: This study evaluates four base algorithms (PC, GES, FCI, GFCI_approx) and three bootstrap-enhanced variants (FGES_boot, GFCI_approx_boot, FCI_boot) under conditions intended to reflect constraints commonly encountered in applied social science research, with the aim of understanding how different causal discovery methods behave under realistic small-sample, low-dimensional, and imperfect-data conditions. Methodology: Algorithms were evaluated through a factorial simulation design spanning small sample sizes (N = 25–200), low dimensionality (p = 4–6), moderate outlier contamination, and moderate departures from linearity (20% of causal relationships generated using quadratic functions), complemented by an illustrative application to a real educational dataset (35 countries × 7 indicators). GFCI_approx is a custom hybrid causal discovery method inspired by selected components of the GFCI framework (Ogarrio et al., 2016); because it replaces the FGES search phase with hill-climbing optimization, it should be interpreted as a distinct hybrid method rather than an implementation of the original GFCI algorithm. Findings: Five main findings emerged. First, all algorithms exhibited a pronounced precision–recall asymmetry, with consistently higher precision (0.92–1.00) than recall (0.43–0.91) across the examined conditions. Second, recovery performance improved substantially between N = 50 and N = 100, while estimation uncertainty decreased and performance became more stable at larger sample sizes. Third, increasing dimensionality (p = 4 to 6) reduced recovery accuracy unless compensated by larger sample sizes. Fourth, moderate outlier contamination reduced performance by approximately 5–22%, whereas the moderate departures from linearity examined in this study had only a negligible effect (approximately 1.6%), indicating that outlier contamination was the dominant source of performance degradation. Fifth, an illustrative application to a real educational dataset showed that, after removal of a composite variable, PC and FCI converged on two undirected edges; bootstrap stability analysis provided stronger support for the Effectiveness–Equity edge (stability = 0.800, 95% CI [0.657, 0.943]) than for the Parent engagement–Effectiveness edge (stability = 0.733, 95% CI [0.575, 0.892]). Because no ground-truth causal structure is available, this application should be interpreted as an illustration of the proposed workflow rather than as a validation of recovered causal relationships. Conclusion & Implications: We provide practical recommendations regarding sample size, preprocessing, and robustness assessment, and argue that causal discovery in small-sample social science settings is best viewed as a conservative hypothesis-generation and filtering tool rather than a comprehensive structure recovery method. By aligning the simulation design with realistic applied constraints, this study helps bridge the gap between methodological research and empirical practice.

Abstract
