Minimax designs and estimators for causal inference under network interference

Vishesh Karwa, Edoardo M. Airoldi · arxiv

We develop a design-based minimax theory for linear causal estimands under interference. We study the minimax risks of three problems: optimizing an estimator under a fixed design, optimizing an estimator and a design from a restricted class, or optimizing both over all designs. In each problem, we study the minimax risk over all estimators and over design-unbiased estimators separately. We allow any correctly specified exposure mapping, including the identity. Our theory represents causal inference under interference as a constrained sampling design on an expanded finite population of $\sum_i d_i$ potential outcomes, where $d_i$ is the number of exposure levels attainable by unit $i$. We describe the constrained sampling design by a weighted sampling hypergraph: its vertices are the potential outcomes, its hyperedges are the maximal sets of potential outcomes that can be jointly sampled under feasible treatment assignments, and its weights are induced by the randomization design. We show that the minimax risks of the fixed-design, restricted-design, and all-design problems depend, respectively, on the weighted hypergraph induced by the design, the collection of weighted hypergraphs attainable within the restricted class, and the unweighted hypergraph. Our Bayes lower bounds compare potential-outcome tables that differ on jointly missing sets of potential outcomes: joint-missingness probabilities govern the lower bounds over all estimators, whereas the corresponding odds govern the lower bounds over unbiased estimators. We illustrate the theory for average treatment effects without interference, saturation designs under partial interference, total, direct, and contagion effects under neighborhood interference, and bipartite dose-response contrasts. The upper and lower bounds match in several settings.

Beyond biased coin minimization: eliminating shift in covariate-adaptive randomization for unequal allocation

Hengjia Fang, Wei Ma · Biometrika

The biased coin has long been a central device in covariate-adaptive randomization: an imbalance measure is defined, and the next assignment is biased toward the treatment that improves balance. Although this biased coin minimization paradigm is highly effective under equal allocation, its natural extensions to unequal allocation can cause a shift problem, where additional covariate imbalances are not centered at zero. We propose a new covariate-adaptive randomization procedure that moves beyond this paradigm by designing the allocation function directly. The resulting procedure achieves strong marginal balance for covariates used in allocation and eliminates the shift asymptotically for additional covariates. The allocation form is derived from a mechanism analysis of the shift problem and its elimination. An explicit variance formula further reveals favorable variability in additional covariate imbalance.

Prior Probabilities for Constrained Hypotheses: Invariance, Neutrality, and Model Search

Joris Mulder · arxiv

When testing hypotheses with equality and inequality constraints using Bayes factors, the hypotheses are commonly assigned equal prior probabilities. The resulting posterior probabilities depend on how the analyst divides the parameter space into hypotheses: splitting one hypothesis into two changes the posterior probabilities of the other hypotheses, and a hypothesis can receive more posterior probability than a hypothesis that contains it. An alternative is considered where the prior probabilities of the hypotheses follow from a single prior distribution on the parameters. Prior probabilities of the equality constrained subspaces are taken from the Bayesian variable selection literature, and prior probabilities of the inequality constrained regions within these subspaces are given by their relative complexities under the encompassing prior. Under this complexity-induced prior, posterior probabilities do not depend on how the hypotheses are divided, are coherent across nested hypotheses, and agree with those from an exploratory model search over the same subspaces. In return, inequality constraints do not increase the posterior probability of a hypothesis relative to the null hypothesis. The prior is therefore suited for analysts who want to test constrained hypotheses without favoring specific constraints a priori, while the potential gain in evidence for the constraints remains quantified by the Bayes factor.

BONuS: Multiple multivariate testing with a data-adaptive test statistic

Zhifei Li, Chiao-Yu Yang, Lihua Lei, and 2 others · arxiv

We propose a new adaptive empirical Bayes framework, the Bag-Of-Null-Statistics (BONuS) procedure, for multiple testing where each hypothesis testing problem is itself multivariate or nonparametric. BONuS is an adaptive and interactive knockoff-type method that helps improve the testing power while controlling the false discovery rate (FDR). The BONuS procedure is closely connected to many existing FDR-controlling methods, such as the counting knockoffs procedure and Benjamini-Hochberg procedure with conformal p-values. Contrary to procedures that start with a $p$-value for each hypothesis, BONuS analyzes the entire data set to adaptively estimate an optimal $p$-value transform based on an empirical Bayes model. Moreover, it can iteratively refine the model fit by peeling off synthetic null statistics to reduce noise in a highly adaptively way. Our method controls FDR in finite samples even if the empirical Bayes model is incorrect or the estimation is poor. An extension, the Double BONuS procedure, validates the empirical Bayes model to guard against power loss due to model misspecification. We demonstrate the power gain of BONuS procedure on multi-trait GWAS, asset pricing, and LLM evaluation.

MediPFN: Amortized In-Context Nuisance Learning and Inference for Causal Mediation Analysis

Shi Bo, Jiaming Hu · arxiv

Causal mediation analysis decomposes a treatment effect into indirect pathways through mediators and direct pathways through other processes. Tabular foundation models perform classification and regression through in-context learning, providing estimates of conditional means and propensity scores used in causal mediation analysis. Constructing confidence intervals from these estimates requires accounting for uncertainty introduced by fitting and combining several nuisance functions. We introduce MediPFN, an amortized inference framework that adapts a pretrained tabular foundation model to nuisance prediction and combines its predictions through a cross-fitted influence-function estimator. A separate head learns quantiles of the estimator's standardized error from simulated tables with known indirect effects. Supervised fine-tuning, symmetrization, and independent calibration then produce a confidence interval. We establish coverage bounds for the resulting intervals under standard assumptions. Across experiments, MediPFN attains coverage closer to the nominal level than several comparators. It also reduces the computational cost of uncertainty quantification, running 59.1 times faster than bootstrap in matched comparisons.

Exact Inference in Fixed-Effect Regressions with Concentrated Identifying Variation

Stanis{\l}aw M. S. Halkiewicz · arxiv

In fixed-effect regressions with many groups, a handful of observations can carry most of the identifying variation left after fixed effects are removed. Conventional $t$-tests can then reject a true null more than half the time. This paper specializes the exact $t$-test of Pouliot to such designs. Contrasts chosen from the design eliminate the nuisance coefficients exactly; sign flips give finite-sample valid tests under independent symmetric errors, or compatible independent symmetric error blocks, allowing arbitrary heteroskedasticity. In two-way designs, the annihilating space is the cycle space of the bipartite observation graph and contains all within variation. This makes the choice of contrasts a graph problem. The observable capture ratio $\kappa_{\mathcal C}\in[0,1]$ measures retained identifying variation and, under diffuse homoskedastic conditions, gives the efficiency relative to an oracle test. Structured packing trades capture against the number and balance of usable signs. The matched-weight test is also asymptotically valid without symmetry when independent centered contrast scores satisfy a fourth-moment negligibility condition. In the Grunfeld investment regression, where one observation carries $73.9\%$ of the realized squared score, 32 contrasts capture $\kappa_{\mathcal C}=0.627$ and give a symmetry-based exact $95\%$ confidence interval of $[0.150, 0.450]$.

A Bayesian approach for checking and estimating block designs in agricultural experiments

Josefina Lacasa, Ignacio A. Ciampitti, Trevor J. Hefley · arxiv

Spatial blocking is a common technique used in designed agricultural experiments. Spatial blocking, however, requires important assumptions about the spatial dependence patterns in the field. Statistical models of blocked agricultural experiments often show spatial structure in the residuals, suggesting that the original model may benefit from additional assumptions regarding the spatial dependence patterns present in the field. We propose a Bayesian model for checking block membership and estimating spatial patterns missing in the original block design. Our model is particularly motivated by cases with mis-specified blocks. To accomplish this, we embed a regression tree within a Bayesian statistical model to estimate spatial effects with a block-like spatial structure. We illustrate the benefits of our model using simulated data mimicking four typical scenarios we have encountered in our applied experience in agricultural research and using two datasets generated from designed agricultural experiments. We found that our model may serve two purposes: validating the originally designed block layout, or mis-specified block-like spatial structures. Thus, our model provides researchers with a novel tool for analyzing data from designed agricultural experiments.

Stand-In Effects and Pooling Amplification: Lessons from Biological World Models for Testing Action Effects

Bowen Xu, Boyu Chen, Jiaying Li · arxiv

Action-conditioned world models are increasingly trained on data from randomized experiments, and whether their predictions respond to actions is usually checked by replacing or removing the actions and measuring the loss in accuracy. Taking biological experiments as the guiding case, we show that such diagnostics report action effects where there are none: under autocorrelated designs, future actions stand in for history the model does not see. When the design law is known, a conditional randomization test written as an e-value is valid in finite samples whatever the model, provided the resampler conditions on the full state the design used. A second failure arises whenever many sites share one action sequence, as in world models: the pooled score turns small model errors along the action contrast into overwhelming evidence against a true effect. In a pixel world, its median log e-value fell from -17.3 to -6832 as frames grew from 64 to 4096 pixels, while the oracle's rose. Tempering the score changes the bet, not the validity. Our own preregistered analysis of optogenetic ERK experiments failed in exactly this way; a tempered statistic, registered before the data were obtained, then rejected the null on a never-opened field (E = 1.1e10). Every single tempering factor we tried, estimated from residuals or tuned on validation data, collapsed in some setting; mixtures over a fixed grid of factors never did. In registered tests on three world-model families trained by others, the pooled test rejected in 0 of 96 units of a diffusion model in which actions mattered, and reached the registered power bar in 10 of 26 Atari games under IRIS's own token likelihood and 1 of 6 control tasks under TD-MPC2; mixtures fixed in advance reached it in all 96 units, 24 of 26 games and 6 of 6 tasks. Under exact nulls the statistics made 4 rejections in 22,848 tests.

Differentially Private Statistical Inference for Rankings from Pairwise Comparisons

Xintao Xia, Jia Gu · arxiv

Reliable inference for rankings of large language models based on human pairwise comparisons must preserve the privacy of individual preference records. To meet this need, we develop methods for score and rank inference under the Bradley-Terry model with edge differential privacy. For an objective-perturbed estimator, a new leave-one-out analysis establishes finite-sample bounds on Euclidean and entrywise estimation errors. For sufficiently dense comparison graphs, the error bounds extend to smaller privacy budgets, including regimes in which its privacy term dominates its statistical term. A uniform asymptotic expansion yields asymptotically valid confidence intervals for individual scores and pairwise score differences. To improve finite-sample performance, we account for both regularization bias and privacy-induced randomness by combining a plug-in ridge-bias correction with Gaussian-Laplace calibration. We further establish a bootstrap procedure for simultaneous rank inference, providing asymptotically valid confidence sets for individual ranks and tests of top-$K$ membership. When the comparison probability is public and known, all proposed inferential procedures require no additional privacy budget. We evaluate finite-sample performance through simulations and apply the proposed methods to rank large language models for creative writing.

Economics of Selection in Ranking & Selection: Neither Fixed-Precision nor Fixed-Budget

Stephen E. Chick, Zaile Li, L. Jeff Hong · arxiv

Ranking and selection (R&S) has evolved tremendously over the last several decades, with applications in agriculture, discrete optimization with computer simulation, clinical trial design, and other areas. We challenge a widely accepted classification of R&S procedures into two categories: fixed-precision and fixed-budget. We discuss a third class, economics-of-selection (EoS) procedures, which is conceptually distinct and is found in the extant literature on Bayesian hypothesis testing and simulation optimization. Procedures in this third class seek to optimally balance two quantities: the cost of learning about the performance of each alternative and the quality of the alternative ultimately selected on the basis of that learning. Fixed-precision and fixed-budget procedures tend to optimize one of these quantities while constraining the other. We clarify these differences and show why they matter. We then present new frequentist R&S procedures in this third category, including both two-stage procedures and a sequential heuristic, assuming Gaussian observations with either known or unknown variances. We find that EoS is not a question of Bayesian versus frequentist analysis, asymptotic versus finite-sample analysis, finite-stage versus fully sequential procedures, or comparing one mean with a standard versus comparing multiple alternatives. We identify opportunities for further R&S research.

Efficient estimation of average causal effect with mismeasured exposures

Zhumei Sui, Min Zeng, Hong Zhang · Statistical Methods in Medical Research

Exposure mismeasurement is commonly seen in causal inference based on observational data. Recent evidence suggests that 35%-61% of medical studies using diagnostic tools report non-negligible exposure mismeasurement. For example, in the Global Enteric Multicenter Study, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA)-based rotavirus detection showed substantial discordance with polymerase chain reaction-based TaqMan Array Card (TAC) testing: 20.1% of ELISA-negative cases tested TAC-positive, whereas 19.5% of ELISA-positive cases tested TAC-negative. The existing methods handling exposure measurement error usually require that the mismeasurement distribution is known or can be estimated from a subset of study subjects with validated exposure statuses. This condition, however, is often violated when the mismeasurement mechanism is complex. In this article, we develop a novel method for estimating average causal effect using samples with exposures subject to misclassification and limited validation subsamples with correctly ascertained exposures. Our method does not require making any modeling assumptions about exposure mismeasurement. We establish some large-sample properties for the proposed method, based on which statistical inference can be easily carried out. The superiority of our proposed estimator is illustrated through extensive simulation studies. Our proposed estimator is shown to achieve a 39%-53% reduction in variance over the estimator relying solely on validation data in our simulation situations. In the application to the Global Enteric Multicenter Study, the proposed method reveals a substantial adjustment of pathogen-specific diarrhea associations that were previously distorted by inaccurate diagnoses.

Knowing When to Intervene: Causal Evaluation of AI Agents

Ayush Sawarni, Wenqian Xing, Tijana Zrnic, and 2 others · arxiv

Current evaluations of agentic AI systems primarily rely on benchmarks. However, the static nature of benchmarks makes them uninformative about the value of auditing interventions at deployment time. For reliable deployment, we often need to determine within an agent's trajectory whether or not to intervene, e.g., by verifying the agent's current output or steering it toward different behavior, using either human review or more costly models or time-consuming agentic supervision. The value of such interventions is fundamentally a causal inference question, asking whether the causal effect of such an intervention is beneficial for the final outcome. Estimating this causal effect is particularly challenging due to the dynamic nature of the problem: the impact of an intervention depends on the current context and environment, as well as the agent's future behavior. We develop the first methodologically grounded framework for evaluating the causal effects of randomized interventions within agent trajectories. By leveraging a relatively small dataset of agent trajectories collected under randomized interventions such as verification or screening, we can estimate the dynamic causal effect of a rich class of (contextual) intervention policies. The structural parameters that we estimate can also be used to construct safety leaderboards that capture the risk of various agentic actions, which can be used by practitioners for audit prioritization. Applying our methods to tau-bench Retail, ToolSandbox, and ImpossibleBench, we find that intervention effects vary substantially across agent actions and states. More broadly, our work suggests a rich research agenda at the intersection of causal inference and agentic evaluation, which we believe is essential for the reliable and safe deployment of AI agents in the real world.

Estimation and inference in penalized function-on-function regression with within-curve residual dependence

Fabian Scheipl · arxiv

Penalized function-on-function regression, as implemented in the R package refund, considers all grid values as independent conditional on the linear predictor, but some dependence typically remains. We study the consequences for estimates and pointwise intervals in a simulation and a plasmode study on nine real datasets. Under dependence, restricted maximum likelihood (REML) undersmooths the coefficient surface: in our simulations' main settings, its relative error is two to four times that of curve-blocked neighborhood cross-validation (NCV). In our plasmode study, both fits have large errors, possibly through weak identification, and NCV is usually, but not always, better. Under dependence, the independence-model-based intervals for means and surfaces undercover severely. We propose and evaluate a curve-clustered sandwich covariance with a leverage adjustment for the penalized hat matrix with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom which brings coverage close to nominal in most settings. Its intervals for function-on-function effects are wide because REML's estimate is noisy. The same construction at the NCV estimate is narrow but undercovers. We therefore recommend fitting twice: NCV for the shape of the surface, REML with these intervals for inference. For estimated means, intercept functions and scalar covariate effects, one REML fit with these intervals usually suffices.

Semiparametric Inference in Panel Data with Interactive and High-dimensional Confounding

Bin Chen, Yukun Ma, Qiyang Yu · arxiv

This paper develops inference for a low-dimensional treatment coefficient in partially linear panel models with high-dimensional observed covariates and latent interactive confounding. Flexible adjustment for observed covariates can leave correlated factor components in treatment and outcome residuals, invalidating standard double/debiased machine learning (DML). We propose Factor-Augmented DML (FA-DML), which estimates treatment-side and reduced-form factor spaces from first-stage residuals. The estimator combines a feasible double projection with three-fold, unit-level cross-fitting. The double projection controls interactions between the two estimated factor spaces. The separate folds eliminate same-training-unit pairings in the leading disturbance components of the factor-estimation errors. Under fixed factor ranks, pervasive factor signals, strict exogeneity, and joint asymptotics in the number of units N and periods T , FA-DML is first-order equivalent to an infeasible oracle, is asymptotically normal, and admits consistent variance estimation. In the baseline simulation design with strong interactive confounding, FA-DML confidence intervals have coverage close to the nominal level. Standard DML and a two-fold variant that estimates both factor spaces on the same units substantially undercover. In an application to U.S. states, FA-DML finds that a 10% increase in the real minimum wage is associated with a roughly 1.1% lower 50/10 wage ratio. The estimate is stable across control sets and first-stage learners.

A Fully Specified Bayes Factor Range for Hypothesis Testing and Sensitivity Analysis in Process Tracing

Matias L\'opez, Jake Bowers, Daniel Gajardo Cooper · arxiv

A Bayes factor can be used in process tracing to reason about the probative value of evidence for a working hypothesis against a rival (Fairfield and Charman 2022). However, relying on the researcher's own sense of probative strength may inflate confidence in $H_1$ (Zaks 2021). We show how to fully specify a generative model of observations to compute Bayes factors in process tracing: First, researchers count the evidence. Second, they classify it as favoring either hypothesis. Third, they formalize a data-generating process that makes the evidence more expected given $H_1$ and another for a rival $H_R$. The ratio of these probabilities yields a fully specified Bayes factor. We compute two Bayes factors under different assumptions, yielding a Bayes factor range that allows researchers to decide whether the balance of evidence favors $H_1$ considering probability thresholds. Our method also facilitates sensitivity analyses of common assumptions, including those about bias and the probative weight of a smoking gun.

Average versus expected causal effects under interference: Modeling assumptions and estimation bottlenecks

Vishesh Karwa, Edoardo M. Airoldi, Donald B. Rubin · arxiv

Causal inference under treatment interference requires assumptions to reduce the exponentially large number of potential outcomes. We develop hierarchical finite-population models for potential outcomes by separating these assumptions into three layers: interference neighborhoods, exposure mappings, and outcome models. Given an interference neighborhood and exposure mapping, we show that potential outcomes admit a nonparametric decomposition into direct, spillover, and treatment--spillover interaction components. This decomposition provides a common coordinate system for nonparametric, semiparametric, and parametric models of finite-population potential outcomes. For a given potential-outcome model, we define two classes of estimands. Average causal effects (ACEs), such as direct and total treatment effects, contrast potential outcomes under fixed treatment--exposure conditions. Expected causal effects (ECEs), such as expected direct and indirect effects, contrast expectations of potential outcomes under a specified allocation policy. For both classes, we bound the variance of the canonical Horvitz--Thompson estimator by three terms: a diagonal weight term, a term from overlapping interference neighborhoods, and a residual design-dependence term. Our upper and lower bounds show that under graph-unaware designs, nonparametric design-unbiased estimation of ACEs can require sample sizes growing exponentially in the interference degree, whereas polynomial growth can suffice for design-aligned ECEs. Under neighborhood interference, we construct graph-aware independent-set designs with variance bounds polynomial in the maximum degree for direct and total treatment effects on every graph, whereas graph cluster randomization can incur worst-case variance exponential in the degree.

ATHOS: Agreement-based Testing of Hypotheses in Overlapping Subgroups

Xiao Wang · arxiv

We study confirmation of treatment benefit in a prespecified family of overlapping subgroups of a randomized trial. Maximum-statistic procedures exploit the correlation between subgroups' statistics, but calibration decides what they protect. Re-randomization controls error exactly when treatment has no effect, but not when only an average effect is zero, whereas a multiplier bootstrap protects average effects only asymptotically. Our first result characterizes when re-randomization alone protects average effects. It reverses the arms' weights in the statistics' covariance, so with unequal allocation and heterogeneous effects it can overstate their correlations; in one such family of 50 subgroups, its limiting familywise error is 0.064 at nominal 0.025. We propose a step-down procedure called ATHOS, short for agreement-based testing of hypotheses in overlapping subgroups, which confirms a subgroup only when both calibrations reject it. Given assignments exchangeable within strata, ATHOS controls familywise error exactly when treatment has no effect and, under complete randomization, asymptotically for average effects, even in large families. Our second result shows that requiring agreement costs no first-order local power in balanced trials. In a null simulation with 200 patients and 1,497 subgroups, the bootstrap step-down alone made false claims in 40% of trials and ATHOS in 2.6%. In two comparisons in an HIV trial, ATHOS confirmed 188 and 73 subgroups and Holm's procedure 179 and 62.

Retrospective causal attribution evaluates whether altering an earlier event would have prevented an observed outcome. Observational data determine marginal potential-response probabilities at fixed inputs while leaving cross-world joint distributions unspecified. Comparing randomized trials: a formal approach

Yu-Han Chiu, Issa J. Dahabreh · arxiv

We sketch a causal framework for comparing the results of randomized trials that explicitly accommodates the possibility of trial engagement effects. Objective This brief report examines German parents’ post-birth employment trajectories, highlighting gender differences in mismatches between work-hour preferences and actual employment. We bridge these two literature streams by using a dynamic approach, illustrating how actual-to-preferred-hour mismatches evolve when the youngest child is aged 0-3. Method We apply multichannel sequence and fuzzy clustering analysis on data from the German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP, 2008-2023), restricting our sample to 645 cohabitating couples. Next, we use Dirichlet regression to link the identified (fuzzy) patterns to individual, household, and contextual characteristics. Results Among mothers, mismatches between actual and preferred working hours increase as children grow older, with many mothers employed in short part-time jobs wishing to work more hours (i.e., underemployment), while most full-time working mothers would prefer to reduce their hours (i.e., overemployment). Among full-time working fathers, a substantial share feels either overemployed or satisfied with their current hours. Dirichlet regression reveals that among fathers, especially job characteristics are associated with overemployment, while individual characteristics and constraints are associated with longer-term preferences for inactivity among mothers. Conclusion Results highlight that overemployment and employment matched with preferences is much more common among parents than underemployment, leaving little room for reallocation of paid and unpaid work within couples.
