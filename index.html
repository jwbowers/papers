Large-deviation efficient confidence intervals for the sub-Gaussian mean

Aaditya Ramdas · Stanford Digital Repository · posted 20 November 2026

We study confidence intervals for the mean of iid observations with known sub-Gaussian variance proxy \(s^2\). The usual Chernoff interval is neither Gaussian-efficient (as $n \to \infty, \alpha$ fixed) nor large-deviation efficient \(n^{-1}\log(1/\alpha_n)\to c\in(0,\infty)\), and it does not adapt to the distribution at hand. Recent work produced the first Gaussian-efficient sub-Gaussian confidence sets, and the current work presents the first large-deviation efficient ones, making instance-optimality feasible and practical. The first of our constructions is a simple discretization and application of the method of types. As the bins refine with sample size, both endpoints converge almost surely to the endpoints of the level-\(c\) set of a population Kullback--Leibler projection. A standard change-of-measure argument shows that every uniformly valid interval has limiting width at least this large. A second construction uses the empirical KL projection (so-called empirical $\mathrm{KL}_\mathrm{inf}$), for which we prove a sharp cutoff of order \(\sqrt{\log(en)}+\log(1/\alpha)\). This shows a distinction from its bounded mean subclass, where recent work has shown that the $\sqrt{\log(en)}$ term can be omitted.

Robust Signal Maximization in Spillover Experiments

Kirill Borusyak, Peter Hull, Evan Munro · arxiv · posted 24 November 2026 · econ.EM

We study the optimal design and analysis of experiments for estimating spillover effects. Assuming a known (e.g., linear) exposure mapping, we characterize the treatment-assignment distribution and regression-based estimator that minimize worst-case approximate variance against a broad class of distributions of unobservables. The design problem yields an intuitive solution in which the planner trades off spillover signal strength against dispersion of spillover variation. The analysis problem yields a simple recentered instrumental variable estimator to best leverage this variation. This framework produces natural solutions in several benchmark cases - such as clustered exposure - and suggests computationally tractable approximations for general networks, including bipartite settings. We illustrate these new tools in semi-synthetic experiments based on three applications from development economics. Our approach yields large standard error reductions in these experiments, increasing effective sample sizes by 44-266%.

Adaptive experiments with observational data: Efficient experimental design and asymptotic time-uniform inference for long-term treatment effects

Kentaro Kawato · arxiv · posted 24 November 2026 · econ.EM, stat.ME

Estimating long-term treatment effects in experiments requires waiting for outcomes to become observable, which can delay both the evaluation of an intervention and subsequent decisions. Recent econometric work has developed identification methods that combine experimental data with observational data containing long-term outcomes. This paper proposes using observational data available before the experiment begins not only for identification but also for designing adaptive experiments, in which experimental data are collected sequentially. To estimate the long-term average treatment effect as precisely as possible, we express its semiparametric efficiency bound as a function of the covariate sampling and treatment assignment rules and minimize the bound over these rules to characterize an optimal fixed experimental design. This experimental design depends on the treatment-specific conditional variances of the observational prediction of the long-term outcome, which can be learned only from experimental participants. We therefore construct a sequential procedure that learns the experimental design as experimental data accumulate and show that its estimator attains the same limiting variance as an oracle estimator implemented under the optimal fixed experimental design. We further construct an asymptotic confidence sequence whose coverage holds simultaneously over all rounds after a prespecified start, so that the experimenter can inspect the interval after each participant and stop once it excludes zero. Monte Carlo simulations show that the learned experimental design can improve precision and shorten the time to detection relative to baseline sampling and equal-probability assignment. An illustration using the Pakistan Graduation experiment shows how estimated conditional variance heterogeneity informs sampling and treatment assignment.

Statistical inference under response-adaptive experimental designs: a systematic review

Haochen Song, Joseph Williams, Kiarash Kianidehkordi, and 4 others · arxiv · posted 24 November 2026 · stat.ME

Response-adaptive designs, including bandit-based approaches, update treatment allocation using accrued outcomes. In this paper, we systematically reviewed how studies in health and biostatistics conducted and evaluated inference under these designs. We searched six databases for studies published from January 2010 through October 2025. Of 774 records identified, 36 studies met the inclusion criteria. We summarized them using study-level counts and structured narrative comparisons, with reporting guided by PRISMA 2020. Most studies focused on methods or designs. Evidence supporting inference came mainly from theory and simulation. Treatment Policy Design in the Presence of Measurement Error

Chang Liu, AmirEmad Ghassami · arxiv · posted 24 November 2026 · stat.ME

In many applications, treatments are assigned based on unit features, leading to personalized treatment policies. This often involves optimizing objective functions with counterfactual quantities such as the conditional average treatment effect (CATE). In practice, some key features may be measured with error and ignoring such errors can introduce systematic bias. We study how to design personalized treatment policies when some features are latent and only noisy measurements are available. We ask how policies can be constructed when the CATE is not identifiable, and how uncertainty from measurement error can be incorporated into policy design. We propose two frameworks for treatment policy design based on partial identification, focusing on the measurement mechanism-the conditional distribution of observed measurements given unobserved features. Treatment policies are then derived by comparing the null value of the CATE to these bounds. The proposed methods provide a coherent way to account for measurement error and model uncertainty in personalized treatment policy design.

Discovery-Targeted False Discovery Rate Control

Motivated by this practical need, we develop an FDR control approach with a data-driven, discovery-targeted level, which uses synthetic null p-values to split the p-value information between level selection and multiple testing. What Should an Experiment Measure? Combining a Short-Term Experiment with Confounded Observational Data to Identify Long-Term Treatment Effects

Kentaro Kawato · arxiv · posted 24 November 2026 · econ.EM, stat.ME

Observational data often record long-term outcomes but do not identify long-term treatment effects when selection depends on unrecorded characteristics, while randomized experiments remove this selection but require years of follow-up. We ask what a short-term experiment should measure so that, combined with observational data, it identifies the average long-term treatment effect in the observational population. In our main setting, the observational data record a coarse post-treatment measurement of the unrecorded characteristics but not a detailed measurement that would remove confounding. In this setting, we derive three implications for what the experiment should measure. It should (1) collect the same coarse measurement, (2) measure the characteristics in enough detail that conditioning on this detailed measurement and recorded covariates removes confounding and leaves the coarse measurement uninformative about selection and the treatment-specific long-term outcome mean, and (3) distinguish no more levels of the characteristics than the coarse measurement can separate. We characterize the treatment effect through linear inverse problems based on exclusion restrictions and completeness, and thereby show that combining such an experiment with the observational data identifies the effect. We propose an estimator that is based on the efficient influence function and removes the first-order effect of nuisance estimation errors. We give conditions under which this estimator is asymptotically normal and attains the semiparametric efficiency bound. Cross-fitting relaxes the conditions on nuisance estimation. In simulations and a Job Corps illustration, the proposed Tikhonov-penalized series estimator has smaller absolute bias and coverage closer to the nominal level than competing estimators.

BACKGROUND: The standardized mean difference (SMD) is a frequently used metric for evaluating covariate balance across treatment status. This work aimed to calculate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for SMDs and compare strategies for selecting covariates for adjustment based on the precision of the SMD in addition to its value. METHODS: We simulated data for an exposure, outcome, and five covariates independent of the exposure in 3 different sample size scenarios (small = 500; medium = 2000; large = 10 000) using 1000 replicates. We calculated the SMD-based imbalance proportion (IP, defined as the proportion of SMDs exceeding the established cutoff of |SMD| > 0.1) and SMD 95% CI-based IP (proportion of 95% CIs that exclude the null). The SMD 95% CI-based IP ranged from 3.7% to 6.3% in all scenarios. In the small scenario, root-mean squared error was largest for the crude RD (0.045), decreasing to 0.041 and 0.034 for RD2 and RD1, respectively. CONCLUSIONS: The proportion of SMDs indicating imbalance increased as sample size decreased. The addition of 95% confidence intervals around SMD estimates may be a useful way to capture SMD variability, though our simulations support the use of the traditional cutoff for variable selection even in the smallest sample size examined.

We propose TRAIL (Tail Reconstruction via Adaptive Iterative Learning), a method that estimates the pooled conditional null under a Gaussian factor model using a Gaussian-Hermite expansion without requiring the null correlation matrix as input. The procedure progressively reconstructs the potentially contaminated tail by generating pseudo-observations and retaining them in successive blocks from the initial cutoff outward. Quantifying and calibrating precision gain from covariate adjustment in clinical trials with binary endpoints

Jannik Hammerschmied, Christian Stock, Martin Schnuerch · arxiv · posted 24 November 2026 · stat.ME

Covariate adjustment is widely used in randomized clinical trials to improve estimation and inference of treatment effects. For continuous endpoints, the reasoning is straightforward: prognostic baseline covariates explain outcome variability and thereby increase precision. For binary endpoints, however, the situation is more complex because conditional and marginal effects generally differ in nonlinear outcome models such as logistic regression. We develop a theoretical framework for quantifying the maximum attainable first-order precision gain from covariate adjustment when estimating marginal treatment effects for binary endpoints. We derive a closed-form identity showing that precision-gain potential is governed by an estimand- and allocation-specific combination of the treatment-specific risk functions. Building on this identity, we introduce marginal prognostic strength as a population-level measure of prognostic information and characterize when it determines precision-gain potential and when the two quantities decouple. Specifically, substantial prognostic information within both treatment arms need not translate into improved precision for the marginal treatment effect when covariates are predictive on the target effect scale. We further develop a calibration algorithm for methodological simulation studies that preserves prespecified conditional outcome structures and marginal risks while allowing data-generating mechanisms to be calibrated by marginal prognostic strength and evaluated in terms of estimand-specific precision-gain potential. Together, these results provide a theoretical and practical framework for understanding, quantifying, and calibrating potential precision gains from covariate adjustment for binary endpoints.

Doubly robust estimation of direct and spillover effects using multiple pre-treatment periods

Martin Huber · arxiv · posted 24 November 2026 · econ.EM, stat.ME

Under interference, the treatment of some units affects the outcomes of others, so that the effect on a treated unit consists of a direct and a spillover component. A non-treated comparison group then generally identifies neither component, but the difference between the total effect on the treated and the spillover effect on the comparison units. Under certain identifying assumptions, both components may be identified separately when multiple pre-treatment periods are available, even in the absence of an uncontaminated comparison group. Under a no-anticipation assumption, pre-treatment outcomes are free of both components for every unit. Extrapolating the pre-treatment trend within a group, as in interrupted time-series approaches, therefore identifies the counterfactual outcome in the absence of any treatment, provided that the model used for the extrapolation is correctly specified. Applied to the treated it gives the total effect on them, applied to the non-treated at a given level of exposure the spillover effect on them. Disentangling the two components in addition requires effect homogeneity across treated and non-treated units with the same exposure and covariates, as well as common support in exposure. We derive doubly robust and Neyman-orthogonal expressions for the effects of interest, which permits controlling for possibly high-dimensional covariates by machine learning. A simulation study confirms the root-$n$ consistent behaviour of the estimators and the reliability of the standard errors under specific regularity conditions. We apply the framework to the compulsory licensing of enemy-owned patents in the United States in 1919. Licensing raised patenting in the licensed subclasses and in technologically close ones alike, so that a comparison-group design understates the total effect.

Identification multiplicity across study designs

Carlos Garc\'ia Meixide, David R\'ios Insua · arxiv · posted 24 November 2026 · stat.ME, math.ST, stat.TH

The answer to a causal question often admits several translations in terms of observed data. These may differ in adjustment sets, comparison groups, instruments, mediators, cutoffs, eligibility occasions, or data sources. We describe the resulting collection of analyses by three structures: the causal target that each analysis identifies under its assumptions, the assumptions that the analyses share, and the sampling dependence among their estimates. A single decomposition separates identification discrepancies from differences in causal targets, and we make both terms explicit in fourteen examples spanning adjustment, comparative panels, proxies, mediators, instruments, discontinuities, staggered adoption, sequential trials, and source combination. Comparisons within a collection can reveal differential violations of assumptions but not a bias common to all analyses; agreement is therefore evidence about the assumptions that differ, not about those that are shared. Averaging retains a causal interpretation in three ways: efficient combination of analyses that identify one target, a projection of a causal curve with a declared approximating family and weighting measure, and inference under weaker premises such as validity of at least one analysis or a known sign of its bias. Precision weights, regression-implied weights, and hierarchical models do not supply these interpretations by themselves. We relate this account to triangulation, overidentification, marginal treatment effects, transportability, and recent methods for inference under model uncertainty, and distinguish identification multiplicity from multiple testing.

How long must an honest confidence interval be after causal discovery? Benign and malignant weak edges

Ruiqi Zhao · arxiv · posted 24 November 2026 · math.ST, math.PR, stat.ME, stat.ML, stat.TH

After causal discovery, a confidence interval for a total effect valid uniformly over the model class must pay for structural uncertainty. We measure this price by the worst-case ratio of expected length to that of the efficient interval knowing the graph, for linear Gaussian models with a known superset of the adjacencies and k-triangle faithfulness. The global price is of order sqrt(n) once one edge can be malignant, so we study it locally, near a generic distribution with one edge absent. Our main result classifies this weak edge by Meek closures. It is benign if deleting it leaves the effect identifiable in the resulting MPDAG and Markov equivalent explanations agree; the local price is then bounded, and otherwise, for small k, of order sqrt(n). At benign edges the price is of order R+K, with R a ratio of efficient lengths and K a cross-model constant. It equals one, so that honesty is free, exactly when K = 0, generically decided by a d-separation. For two Markov classes it is determined within a factor 3.40, and its upper bound is approached without knowing the base point or the weak edge. A further graphical condition gives the rate uniformly in a second edge's margin.

Artificial intelligence has the potential to transform how public services are delivered, with important implications for public service productivity. But how widely is the technology actually being used - and is it making any difference? Our survey of 3,362 public sector workers across the UK, the USA, South Africa and the EU finds widespread use of AI in every region. Our findings suggest that this spread is being driven by bottom-up individual adoption rather than top-down institutional adoption.
