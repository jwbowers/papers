Treatment effect estimation from observational data in healthcare: study designs, inherent biases, and a propensity score-focused quality assessment scale

Sangeeth P. Das, Sabrina Casucci, Forough Mahpouya, Alexander V. Nikolaev · Health Services and Outcomes Research Methodology

Abstract Observational data are widely used to understand treatment-effect relationships, particularly in healthcare, when randomized controlled trials are infeasible. Observational studies, however, are susceptible to biases arising from study design, data limitations, and analytic methods. Identifying and addressing these biases is critical and challenging in observational research. This paper elaborates on the sources of such biases; reviews commonly used observational study designs and propensity score (PS)-based methods; and examines existing tools for assessing the quality of evidence from causal studies under the counterfactual inference paradigm. We argue that, although PS-based methods are widely used, existing quality assessment tools place insufficient emphasis on data quality and inference validity and may overlook sources of bias specific to PS-based analyses. To address this gap, we introduce the PSM-Qual Score, a quality assessment tool that evaluates biases related to defining and identifying comparison groups, de-confounding, and interpretability. We also developed an implementation Guideline to support consistent application of the tool. Two independent assessors evaluated inter-rater reliability across 127 published studies using the PSM-Qual Score and Guideline. Agreement ranged from 82.0% to 99.2%, with Cohen’s kappa values from 0.377 to 0.922 and weighted kappa values from 0.382 to 0.958, with most items demonstrating substantial to almost-perfect agreement. The PSM-Qual Score and Guideline support researchers in the exact and computational sciences in using observational causal inference methods appropriately, contributing to data-based healthcare research.

Interpretable Uplift Modelling: Conditional Average Treatment Effect Attribution, Subgroup Rules and Constrained Recourse

Yu Zhang, Yueguang Shan, Wanlong Ma · Expert Systems

ABSTRACT Uplift models estimate how treatment effects vary across individuals, but a prediction alone does not explain feature contributions, define usable subgroups, or suggest alternative feature states. We combine these three outputs through one fitted conditional average treatment effect (CATE) model. Treatment Effect Shapley Values (TESV) applies existing interventional Shapley attribution directly to this model. Stable Causal Rule Learning (SCRL) produces concise subgroup descriptions using empirical treatment–control support checks and a bootstrap‐based score. Constrained feature‐space recourse searches for higher predicted CATE under permitted coordinate changes. Across four benchmarks, we evaluate these outputs against known effect modifiers, the fitted model, and held‐out outcome contrasts, keeping these reference targets distinct. Attribution reflects dependencies learned by the model; marginal replacement does not remove learned proxy dependence. Rule quality varies with the dataset and evaluation target, and the Hillstrom randomised evaluation does not establish higher per‐contact efficacy than the model's ranking. The framework therefore supports model inspection, while its outputs require further evidence before guiding interventions. Empirical support checks do not establish causal identification, and recourse targets are neither validated actions nor guaranteed jointly plausible or reachable states.

The Goldilocks Regression Discontinuity Design: A Dual-Threshold Framework for Evaluating Interventions Outside an Optimal Range

Brian G. Moss · Evaluation Review

Regression discontinuity designs (RDDs) are widely used to estimate causal effects when treatment assignment is determined by a threshold on a continuous running variable. Many interventions, however, target individuals at both ends of a continuum, with treatment assigned when values fall either below a lower threshold or above an upper threshold. This paper introduces the Goldilocks Regression Discontinuity Design (GRDD), a dual-threshold framework for evaluating such interventions. GRDD estimates two locally identified treatment effects, one at each threshold, while allowing both effects to be interpreted within a common intervention framework. Separate local, simultaneous, and spline-based estimation strategies are presented. A 10-condition Monte Carlo simulation evaluates estimator performance across differences in sample size, functional form, heteroskedasticity, running-variable density, and treatment-effect magnitude. Results demonstrate an important tradeoff between precision and robustness. Simultaneous estimation provides substantial precision gains when the common functional form is correctly specified but can produce substantial bias and poor confidence interval coverage under misspecification. Separate local estimation is more consistently robust across conditions, while spline-based estimation was generally robust under nonlinear relationships. A semi-synthetic application using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) illustrates implementation of the framework at lower and upper body mass index thresholds. GRDD extends the application of RDD to dual-threshold interventions while preserving local identification at each cutoff and providing a unified framework for comparing intervention effects across distinct regions of need.

Target Trial Emulation: Considerations for Physical Therapy Research

Kevin H. McLaughlin, Kelly N. Daley, Amanda McQueen, and 2 others · Physical Therapy

Abstract Randomized clinical trials (RCTs) are considered the ideal method for assessing the effectiveness of physical therapist interventions. However, RCTs are resource-intensive and usually take several years to complete, often providing insights well after they are needed to inform practice. Due to the constantly evolving nature of physical therapy, study designs are needed that can more rapidly generate evidence that can be used to guide clinical care. Target trial emulation (TTE) provides a framework that improves researchers’ abilities to draw causal inferences from observational data, by specifying protocol components of a hypothetical RCT and emulating those components with observational data elements. By leveraging data generated through routine care encounters, TTE allows researchers to examine the effectiveness of interventions as they are being used in clinical care – enhancing researchers’ abilities to generate evidence when it is most needed. However, TTE has not been used extensively among physical therapy researchers, potentially due to a lack of familiarity with this concept or challenges related to the way physical therapy is delivered or the types of data generated during physical therapist encounters. In this manuscript, the authors provide an introduction to the TTE framework, using an ongoing study examining the effectiveness of remote therapeutic monitoring as a case example. The authors also discuss considerations and challenges associated with TTE studies within the context of physical therapy and recommend strategies for addressing these challenges. They also discuss how TTE studies can be enhanced through the addition of implementation-focused data collection and conclude with a discussion of limitations associated with TTE studies.

Two‐stage least squares as a mean and covariance structure estimator: Reconciling econometric and psychometric formulations

Alberto Maydeu‐Olivares, Yves Rosseel · British Journal of Mathematical and Statistical Psychology

Abstract Instrumental variables regression is increasingly used in psychology and is the basis of model‐implied instrumental variables structural equation modelling (MIIV‐SEM), yet econometric and psychometric formulations use different statistical representations and terminology. We develop a general theory of multi‐stage weighted least squares estimation for mean and covariance structures and show that classical two‐stage least squares (2SLS) is the second stage of a three‐stage procedure. The first stage estimates predictive equations for the explanatory variables; the second estimates the structural intercept and regression coefficients; and the third estimates the remaining disturbance variance and covariances, propagating uncertainty from earlier stages. For structural parameters, the normal‐theory and asymptotically distribution‐free covariance estimators reduce exactly to conventional homoscedastic and heteroscedasticity‐robust 2SLS estimators. With model‐based covariance matrices, Browne's normal‐theory goodness‐of‐fit statistic equals Sargan's overidentification statistic, and its ADF counterpart equals Wooldridge's heteroscedasticity‐robust score statistic. Third‐stage disturbance covariances quantify endogeneity and yield Wald tests asymptotically equivalent, under the null, to the Hausman OLS–2SLS contrast and Wooldridge's robust exogeneity test, respectively. The results place MIIV‐SEM estimators, standard errors, and tests within a coherent multi‐stage statistical theory.

From Heterogeneous Treatment Effects to Treatment Policies: A Decision Focused Framework for Treatment Personalization in Drug Development

Nan Miles Xi, Xin Huang, Lin Wang · Statistics in Biopharmaceutical Research

Clinical trials often report evidence that treatment effects vary across patients, but such evidence does not show whether an individualized treatment policy will improve decisions. We propose a two-stage framework that separates treatment effect assessment from policy evaluation. Stage 1 addresses a prespecified treatment effect claim on a defined causal scale, using an analysis and multiplicity strategy suited to that claim. Stage 2 separately estimates treatment effect scores and evaluates candidate policies against a defined comparator through ranking, policy value, incremental value, validation, and empirical surrogate benefit and harm metrics. A Stage 1 result may motivate Stage 2, but it does not validate a policy or determine the inferential status of Stage 2. Simulations covering no effect, constant benefit without HTE, quantitative HTE, and qualitative HTE show that heterogeneity supports personalization mainly when it changes the preferred treatment. Additional simulations examine sample size, candidate covariate dimension, and learner complexity. An AIDS Clinical Trials Group (ACTG) 175 secondary analysis illustrates that population level HTE evidence can coexist with little improvement in held out policy value. The framework provides setting-specific guidance for matching methods, validation, and reporting to distinct treatment effect and policy claims.

An Experimental Design for Confirmatory Clinical Trials Testing the Clinical Utility of an Individualization Rule for 2 Treatments

Francisco J. Díaz, Byron J. Gajewski, Matthew S. Mayo · Medical Decision Making

There is a need for efficient and scientifically sound clinical trials that examine the clinical utility of individualized treatment rules (ITRs) that individualize 2 or more medical treatments. These are trials assessing the effectiveness of the medical decision process taking place in the individualization of the treatments, not the effectiveness of the particular treatments involved. We developed a new experimental design for confirming the superiority of an ITR for 2 treatments over decision processes that ignore patient covariates. The design is built on a new published idea, the ITR's individualization potential (IP), which compares the average effect on clinical response (AER) of the ITR with that of treatment without individualization (TWI). The AER of a decision approach compares the expected potential response of a patient treated with the treatment recommended by the approach versus the expected response should the patient be treated with the alternative therapy instead. The IP concept implies a novel way of constructing the control group of our design and testing the ITR's utility. The experimental design provides estimates of the AERs of both the ITR and TWI, which we use to test the individualization potential's statistical significance. In personalized medicine, one of the 2 treatments is often recommended to a subpopulation of lower-frequency patients more difficult to recruit. Thus, as another innovation, our formulation implies convenient sample size calculations that minimize the number of these patients in the total sample size, contrary to traditional methods of powering clinical trials that usually oversample them. Our design usually needs smaller sample sizes than popular designs and has a more appropriate control group. We illustrate calculating sample sizes for a hypothetical ophthalmology clinical trial.

Heterogeneous treatment effect estimation with instrumental variable methods

Amirhossein Kazemi, Joseph Sexton, Inge Christoffer Olsen · Statistical Methods in Medical Research

Instrumental variable (IV) methods are widely used to address unmeasured confounding in observational studies but typically estimate the local average treatment effect (LATE) for compliers. If there exist effect modifying variables that also affect the choice of treatment, the LATE may have limited clinical relevance. To address this limitation, we propose a novel framework for estimating the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) for a categorical treatment variable in the presence of both observed confounding and effect-modifying variables in addition to valid IVs. Building on recent developments in data fusion between randomized trial and observational data, we combine IV estimators with estimators based on covariate adjustment. By making parametric assumptions about the residual unobserved confounding effect, we obtain a consistent estimator of the CATE with relatively high efficiency. In a simulation study with nonlinear effect modification and unobserved confounding, we show that our estimator outperforms competing IV and covariate-adjusted estimators in terms of mean squared error. We further illustrate the applicability of the method, utilizing it to compare tumor necrosis factor alpha inhibitors as the first line biologic treatment of rheumatoid arthritis conditional on age. The analysis illustrates how the proposed method can estimate population-relevant, clinically interpretable treatment effects and suggests smaller differences between treatments among older patients. This work provides a general framework for combining instrumental and confounding information to estimate individualized causal effects when treatment effects are heterogeneous and there are more than two treatment alternatives.

Robert Andersen, Anders Holm · Human Resource Management Journal

ABSTRACT Although mediation analysis is widely used in human resource management to explain how practices shape outcomes, most cross‐sectional approaches rely on the strong assumption of sequential ignorability. We develop a two‐period difference‐in‐differences framework that accommodates time‐invariant unobserved confounders of the mediator and outcome. Rather than requiring independence in levels, identification rests on restrictions on change, including parallel untreated trends and change‐based mediator–outcome exchangeability. The framework clarifies the assumptions needed to identify total and mediator effects as distinct from those required for controlled direct and natural indirect effects. With observed heterogeneity, the natural indirect effect equals the treated‐population average product of the mediator's effect on the outcome and the treatment's effect on the mediator; the full natural indirect effect adds an unidentified residual covariance term, assessed through sensitivity analysis. We extend the method to multi‐period staggered designs and provide estimation, bootstrap inference, diagnostics, and an open‐source R package.

Takahiro Okuma, Minoru Ishige, Shuichi Kikuchi · PLoS ONE

In observational research, statistical detectability, robustness to adjustment, and predictive utility are distinct properties of a candidate predictor, yet they are often conflated. A statistically significant association may weaken after accounting for co-varying factors and may add little to out-of-sample prediction. We illustrate this distinction using atmospheric pressure and daily attendance at a Japanese psychiatric day-care as a worked example. Using four consecutive fiscal years of facility-level daily attendance, we evaluated same-day atmospheric pressure as a pre-specified candidate predictor in three stages: statistical detectability, robustness to adjustment for co-varying meteorological factors and seasonality, and incremental out-of-sample predictive utility beyond institutional scheduling variables and recent attendance history. More broadly, this worked example illustrates why candidate predictors should be evaluated separately for detectability, robustness of effect estimates, and out-of-sample predictive utility before statistically significant associations are interpreted as meaningful or actionable.

Qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) requires thresholds to construct sets from continuous scales, but a standard analysis is fixed to a single set of thresholds and does not directly reveal how the sufficiency structure changes when the target level of the outcome or the judgment criterion for a condition changes. This article introduces the R package ThSQCA, which uses the QCA package as its computational engine and provides a common workflow to run, aggregate, visualize, and report analyses that systematically vary the outcome threshold, the condition thresholds, or both. Using simulated data constructed for methodological illustration, we show that raising the target level of the outcome narrows the result from multiple sufficient paths to a single path (with lower consistency and higher coverage) and eventually yields no solution. We also show that varying the judgment criterion for a condition can make a solution disappear or produce a solution supported by very few cases. ThSQCA does not select an appropriate threshold automatically; it is a diagnostic workflow that clarifies the range over which a conclusion holds and the points at which it changes.

A Simulation and Real-Data Evaluation of Causal Discovery Algorithms under Realistic Social Science Conditions

Sacha Varin · Asian Journal of Probability and Statistics

Background: Causal discovery algorithms aim to infer causal structure from observational data under explicit assumptions, yet their performance under realistic social science conditions remains insufficiently understood. Existing evaluations typically rely on large samples, high-dimensional settings, and clean data—conditions that rarely hold in applied research. Objectives: This study evaluates four base algorithms (PC, GES, FCI, GFCI_approx) and three bootstrap-enhanced variants (FGES_boot, GFCI_approx_boot, FCI_boot) under conditions intended to reflect constraints commonly encountered in applied social science research, with the aim of understanding how different causal discovery methods behave under realistic small-sample, low-dimensional, and imperfect-data conditions. Methodology: Algorithms were evaluated through a factorial simulation design spanning small sample sizes (N = 25–200), low dimensionality (p = 4–6), moderate outlier contamination, and moderate departures from linearity (20% of causal relationships generated using quadratic functions), complemented by an illustrative application to a real educational dataset (35 countries × 7 indicators). GFCI_approx is a custom hybrid causal discovery method inspired by selected components of the GFCI framework (Ogarrio et al., 2016); because it replaces the FGES search phase with hill-climbing optimization, it should be interpreted as a distinct hybrid method rather than an implementation of the original GFCI algorithm. Findings: Five main findings emerged. First, all algorithms exhibited a pronounced precision–recall asymmetry, with consistently higher precision (0.92–1.00) than recall (0.43–0.91) across the examined conditions. Second, recovery performance improved substantially between N = 50 and N = 100, while estimation uncertainty decreased and performance became more stable at larger sample sizes. Third, increasing dimensionality (p = 4 to 6) reduced recovery accuracy unless compensated by larger sample sizes. Fourth, moderate outlier contamination reduced performance by approximately 5–22%, whereas the moderate departures from linearity examined in this study had only a negligible effect (approximately 1.6%), indicating that outlier contamination was the dominant source of performance degradation. Fifth, an illustrative application to a real educational dataset showed that, after removal of a composite variable, PC and FCI converged on two undirected edges; bootstrap stability analysis provided stronger support for the Effectiveness–Equity edge (stability = 0.800, 95% CI [0.657, 0.943]) than for the Parent engagement–Effectiveness edge (stability = 0.733, 95% CI [0.575, 0.892]). Because no ground-truth causal structure is available, this application should be interpreted as an illustration of the proposed workflow rather than as a validation of recovered causal relationships. Conclusion & Implications: We provide practical recommendations regarding sample size, preprocessing, and robustness assessment, and argue that causal discovery in small-sample social science settings is best viewed as a conservative hypothesis-generation and filtering tool rather than a comprehensive structure recovery method. By aligning the simulation design with realistic applied constraints, this study helps bridge the gap between methodological research and empirical practice.

Signed unbiased estimation without common exposure support

Zihuan Xu · Zenodo (CERN European Organization for Nuclear Research)

This technical note studies whether one assignment-weighted linear estimator of a full-population exposure mean can remain design-unbiased under every graph in a finite candidate set. Exposure records own treatment and the presence of a treated neighbor; the true candidate graph is fixed throughout the experiment and response tables may be heterogeneous. Within the existing design-based Riesz framework, the note presents the finite affine-support condition and its nonnegative common-support corollary, then computes the support structure of candidate perfect matchings. Under uniform complete randomization with exactly two treated units, signed linear unbiased estimation of the (1,1) population mean is feasible exactly when every unit has a person who is its partner in none of the candidate matchings. For a specified four-unit, two-candidate matching experiment with positive treated-pair probabilities p_e and response values in [-M,M], the note establishes uniqueness in the common deterministic linear unbiased class and exact worst design variance R(p)=(M^2/4) sum_e 1/p_e. Uniform treated-pair assignment minimizes this risk at 9M^2; the known-graph linear unbiased oracle value under the same uniform experiment is 3M^2. These costs refer to the stated experiment and estimator classes. Version v1 is the first public technical-note release.

For time-to-event outcomes, Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting (IPCW) method and Rank Preserving Structural Failure Time model (RPSFTM) have been proposed to address treatment switch across two arms, with treatment effects summarised in terms of hazard ratio (HR) or restricted mean survival time (RMST). In this paper, we adapt the IPCW and RPSFTM methods to consider one-sided non-compliance affecting the experimental arm. For IPCW, non-compliers in the experimental arm were censored at the time of non-compliance. For RPSFTM, the causal model was modified to reconstruct the counterfactual survival time for non-compliers in the experimental arm. We quantify the treatment effects using HR, difference (dRMST) or ratio (rRMST) of RMST and milestone survival difference (MSD). We compare the aforementioned methods via rigorous simulation and a real-world data analysis based on a randomised trial of nasopharyngeal cancer. The simulation results demonstrated that RPSFTM-related models produced smaller bias, whereas IPCW generally performed well under high non-compliance for all summary measures. However, the RMST-based estimates had lower power than the HR estimate under idealised proportional hazard setting. When illustrated using real-world data, RPSFTM-related models yielded larger estimate of treatment effect for summary measures of dRMST and rRMST, whereas IPCW produced larger estimates when quantified in terms of HR and MSD. For handling treatment non-compliance in RCTs, it is important to choose the summary measure and model carefully on a case-by-case basis.

Saanvi Chawla, Vasilis Syrgkanis · arxiv

Interference between participants in two-sided marketplace experiments can severely bias conventional A/B tests. While two-sided randomization (TSR), which randomizes both supply and demand, enables estimators that mitigate this bias, existing analyses offer limited guidance on which estimator to prefer for the balanced market conditions common in practice, as they largely focus on extreme supply- or demand-constrained regimes. We introduce the Double Diagonal (DD) estimator, a novel method for interference-robust treatment effect estimation. The DD estimator contrasts the outcomes of treated-treated and control-control pairs and uses the off-diagonal interactions (treated-control and control-treated) to correct for interference-induced bias. Extensive numerical experiments in realistic market settings--spanning heterogeneous treatment effects, supply-demand imbalances, and varied market structures--corroborate our theoretical findings. Across a wide range of configurations, the DD estimator demonstrates the lowest bias, establishing it as a robust and often superior choice for reliable experimentation in two-sided marketplaces.

We introduce a general class of causal estimands that, as a special case, includes the average causal effect of assigning treatment according to an arbitrary counterfactual distribution. Balancing estimators are particularly attractive tools for covariate adjustment in this setting because they avoid directly modeling the treatment assignment mechanism, thereby reducing the potential for instability and misspecification bias in subsequent causal effect estimation. Simulation studies support our theoretical results, and we illustrate the proposed method by estimating the effect of vocational training hours on expected future earnings using data from the U.S. Job Corps study.
