Log-optimal independence testing by betting

Aaditya Ramdas · Stanford Digital Repository · posted 14 November 2026

We construct anytime-valid tests of independence whose log wealth per observation converges to mutual information for every joint distribution on a pair of standard Borel spaces. The same data-driven procedure works without knowledge of either marginal distribution or a common dominating measure. A conditional predictive likelihood, normalized by the maximum marginal likelihood, gives an e-process in the full observation filtration. Refining finite partitions and multiclass kernel learning yield universal attainment; arbitrary probabilistic classifiers give practical implementations that can be trained throughout the test. For two complex data types, a normalized sequential assignment rule gives an exact matching e-value with quadratic score-evaluation cost. We connect these constructions to classical fixed-sample efficiency. Experiments compare practical implementations with sequential kernelized and classifier-based tests, permutation HSIC, and held-out classifier tests.

The cyclic selection problem of conditional inference

Aaditya Ramdas · Stanford Digital Repository · posted 14 November 2026

Conditional selective inference adjusts an interval for the event that led a scientist to examine it. But the scientist may then select again, keeping only the adjusted intervals that exclude zero. A further conditional adjustment can change that selection, inviting another adjustment. This difficulty has been recognized in earlier work. We give an elementary analysis for independent Gaussian observations, using equal-tailed intervals and conditioning on the complete history of reporting decisions. If all coordinates remain available for reconsideration, then, for every finite mean vector—including every vector of positive means—every finite pattern of reporting sets recurs infinitely often almost surely. The procedure reaches an empty set almost surely if stopped there; if continued, empty and nonempty sets both return forever. If only retained coordinates are ever revisited, the report becomes permanently empty almost surely. The continuing procedure’s long-run average number of reports is independent of the signal strengths. An ordinary random draw illustrates four successive complete replacements. We then present the established marginal alternative: the Benjamini–Yekutieli false coverage rate adjustment, with selection designed jointly with the intervals, gives the largest compatible collection of zero-excluding intervals within a chosen marginal family, and a procedure that terminates.

The stable false discovery rate

Aaditya Ramdas · arXiv (Cornell University) · posted 14 November 2026

When a discovery depends on analysis choices, a list of selected hypotheses that controls the false discovery rate (FDR) gives little indication of how easily the finding could change: the selected set may be very sensitive to a variety of analyst choices, like the target FDR level, which machine learning algorithms are used in the pipeline, the choice of test statistics, and so on. We propose that scientists should measure and control a new metric called the \emph{stable FDR} (sFDR), and give methods to control it, including simple wrappers around existing FDR analysis pipelines. sFDR is a natural continuous extension of the FDR: we assign each hypothesis an allocation between zero and one, and the sFDR equals the expected fraction of null allocation to total allocation. If one runs multiple analyses of the same data by varying different reasonable choices, allocations can be determined by simply dividing each run's selection indicators by its discovery count, then averaging across analyses and rescaling. The resulting sFDR equals the average of the ordinary FDRs of the runs, even though the runs are dependent: thus sFDR control holds under the same assumptions that the original FDR control held. (In contrast, naively reporting just fractions of times each variable was selected need not control the sFDR.) It is interpretable: hypotheses that were always selected are allocated 1 and those never selected are allocated 0. In leukemia expression and neuroimaging analyses, findings with larger allocations were more often supported in separate samples. We also develop procedures that can assign stable allocations directly from p-values, e-values, or knockoff statistics, resulting in stable-BH, stable e-BH and stable knockoff procedures. A representation by e-values describes which allocation rules control the error rate and gives ways to optimize allocations for different follow-up goals.

Log-optimal two-sample testing by betting

Aaditya Ramdas · arXiv (Cornell University) · posted 14 November 2026

For each standard Borel observation space, we construct a single anytime-valid two-sample procedure that is asymptotically log-optimal for every pair of unknown probability laws and every limiting ratio of diverging sample sizes, including zero and infinity. For independent balanced pairs, the optimal log-wealth rate is $2\JS(P,Q)$, where $\JS$ is the Jensen--Shannon divergence. Unequal sizes yield weighted Jensen--Shannon information and the one-sample limit $\log W(n_1,n_2)/n_2\to\KL(Q\|P)$ when $n_1/n_2\to\infty$. Refining finite partitions and logarithmic-loss learning with measurable characteristic kernels provide universal constructions. Our main contribution is the generality of our construction, and its connections to classical batch efficiency notions. Practical implementations allow continuous classifier fine-tuning, as demonstrated on finite alphabets, Gaussian data, and handwritten digits.

Randomization protects the average, not the trial: chance covariate imbalance and a calibration of balance $P$ values at the design stage

William J. Dwyer, David Zurakowski · arxiv · posted 19 November 2026 · stat.ME

Randomization makes the unadjusted treatment-effect estimate unbiased over the allocations a scheme could produce, although it does not balance any single trial. Chance imbalances on strongly prognostic baseline variables directly threaten the validity and regulatory credibility of trial results, yet what design-stage balance $P$ values imply for bias and error rates is seldom stated. We review evidence on chance covariate imbalance and derive that link under a linear model with one normal prognostic covariate, with simulation checks. Given the realized allocation, the unadjusted estimate is biased by $\beta$ times the difference in covariate means; in standard-error units the bias is $\rho Z_{\mathrm{bal}}$, where $\rho$ is the covariate-outcome correlation and $Z_{\mathrm{bal}}$ the balance z-statistic, so it does not shrink as trials grow. Pre-specified ANCOVA removes this bias, change scores reverse it, and non-collapsibility attenuates odds and hazard ratios even under perfect balance. A worst-balance threshold of $P = 1/e$ marks where the Sellke-Bayarri-Berger bound reaches 1; under complete randomization the worst of $k$ independent balance $P$ values falls below it with probability $1-(1-1/e)^k$, 0.99 for $k=10$. Any balance $P$ value $\geq 0.317$ keeps the two-sided conditional type I error of the unadjusted test at or below 5% for every $\rho$; at $1/e$ the one-sided error in the favoured direction is at most 4.1%, against 13-33% at $P = 0.05$. Design-stage balance scores should be benchmarked against complete randomization, weighted by external prognostic strength, and reported alongside allocation predictability and a pre-specified covariate-adjusted analysis plan.

Under network interference, outcomes depend on both own treatment and neighbors' treatments, making causal effects functions of neighborhood exposure rather than single treatment contrasts. Existing methods primarily target prespecified causal contrasts or their estimation precision, leaving global response-function recovery largely unexplored. Simulations and an online-game experiment show that FANCI improves recovery of nonlinear exposure-dependent effects and reveals treatment patterns obscured when network interference is ignored.

On the Validity of Fisher Randomization Tests under the Weak Null

Timothy Sudijono, Edgar Dobriban, Eric Tchetgen Tchetgen · arxiv · posted 19 November 2026 · stat.ME, math.ST, stat.TH

Consider the problem of testing the Neyman weak null of no sample average treatment effect $H_{0N}:\tau = 0$ in randomized experiments with binary outcomes. Although the Fisher Randomization Test (FRT) is typically justified under Fisher's sharp null of no individual treatment effect, we show that it is finite-sample-valid for the weak null $H_{0N}$ in three common combinations of study design and test statistic: 1) balanced completely randomized experiments with the difference-in-means test statistic, 2) matched pairs experiments with the same test statistic, and 3) balanced Bernoulli randomized experiments with the centered Horvitz-Thompson test statistic. As an immediate corollary, Fisher's exact test in a balanced completely randomized experiment is valid under the weak null, and so is McNemar's test in a matched pairs experiment. Similar results hold for the one-sided null $H_0:\tau \leq 0$. Beyond balanced designs, we show that twice the one-sided FRT $p$-value with the same test statistics is valid under $H_{0N}$. In simulation experiments, the FRTs compare favorably to other exact procedures.

Estimand frameworks for causal inference in clinical research and regulatory decision making

Miguel A. Hern\'an, Mats J. Stensrud, Xabier Garc\'ia-Alb\'eniz, and 2 others · arxiv · posted 19 November 2026 · stat.ME

Causal inference for clinical and regulatory decision making requires a precise definition of the causal estimand, that is, what we want to estimate. Three frameworks are commonly used to describe causal estimands: PICO and its extensions, the target trial framework, and the E9(R1) Addendum of the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH). Because these frameworks were developed by different scientific communities, they operate at different levels of detail, emphasize different aspects of the causal estimand, and use different terminology for closely related concepts. Here we compare the three frameworks and translate across their terminologies. PICO is a compact framework that helps jumpstart a conversation about the causal question, but it does not encode sufficient information to fully characterize a causal estimand. The target trial framework characterizes the causal estimand precisely by specifying the components of the protocol of the randomized trial that would answer the causal question: eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, assignment, outcome, start and end of follow-up, and causal contrast. E9(R1) starts from intercurrent events and lists strategies to address them, some of which describe estimators rather than estimands, or imply estimands that are not relevant to decision makers or cannot be estimated under reasonable assumptions. We argue that the target trial framework subsumes the other two, and we provide a map of the correspondences across the three frameworks.

Confounding Heterogeneity in Multivariate Sensitivity Analysis for Observational Studies

Anthony Frazier, Siyu Heng, Wen Zhou · arxiv · posted 19 November 2026 · stat.ME

In observational studies, unmeasured confounding may bias causal conclusions even after matching on measured confounders. Sensitivity analysis is therefore usually needed to assess the robustness of causal conclusions to unmeasured confounding. We consider multivariate sensitivity analysis that assesses the causal effect of a single treatment on multiple outcomes. We identify an inherent feature of such analyses, which we call ``confounding heterogeneity'': different outcomes may be affected by distinct unmeasured confounders, and a common unmeasured confounder may affect different outcomes to varying degrees. Building on matched observational studies, we formulate sensitivity analysis frameworks that accommodate confounding heterogeneity. We show that Type I error control for many existing sensitivity analysis methods is guaranteed under sensitivity analysis models and modes of inference that differ fundamentally from those used in the classic framework. We study asymptotic power using design sensitivity and Bahadur-Rosenbaum efficiency and examine finite-sample power through numerical studies. Our results show that the structure of unmeasured confounding affects sensitivity analysis, and that widely used single-parameter frameworks may mischaracterize the sensitivity of a causal conclusion to unmeasured confounding when confounding heterogeneity is considered. Simulations and a data application illustrate the practical importance of confounding heterogeneity in multivariate sensitivity analyses.

We study inference on a treatment effect in a partially linear model whose high-dimensional controls are observed only through repeated noisy measurements. Replacing the controls by the average of their measurements shifts the double machine learning (DML) moment by a bilinear form in the nuisance coefficients and the measurement-error covariance, a bias that better first stages cannot remove. Within-unit differences among the measurements identify this covariance, which need not be diagonal, without reconstructing the latent controls. Covariance-Recalibrated DML (CORE) subtracts the estimated shift from a cross-fitted score and, because the result is not orthogonal to the covariance, accounts for covariance estimation in its standard error. In simulations the correction removes most of the bias.

Balancing Partition Forest for Causal Weighting

The ideal weight is the ratio between the product of the covariate and treatment marginals and their joint distribution, for binary and continuous treatments alike. We propose Balancing Partition Tree (BPT) and Balancing Partition Forest (BPF), which estimate this ratio on a learned rectangular partition of covariate--treatment space. Global Fr\'echet regression generalizes linear regression to such general settings, however statistical inference has remained largely unexplored. Lost in the Bins: Treatment Effects with Discretized Covariates

Cenchen Liu · arxiv · posted 13 November 2026

Mean independence restrictions are widely used to identify average treatment effects, but the covariates used to formulate these restrictions are often observed or used in coarsened form. This paper shows that mean independence conditional on a finely measured covariate need not hold after the covariate is replaced by a coarsened version. Coarsening can therefore change the identifying content of the comparison rather than only the precision of estimation. I characterize the resulting coarsening bias for the ATT and ATE. For the ATT, the bias is driven by within-cell composition in untreated potential outcomes. For the ATE, an additional treatment effect heterogeneity term appears. I then introduce $c$-mean dependence, a nonparametric relaxation of exact mean independence at the coarsened level, and derive sharp bounds for average treatment effects. The parameter $c$ determines how far comparisons may extend across neighboring values of the coarsened covariate. I propose sample analogue estimators and bootstrap inference procedures for the resulting bound functions. In an application to voluntary military service using data from Angrist (1998), positive effects on early earnings, which primarily reflect military pay, hold even under the weakest form of $c$-mean dependence, whereas the evidence on later effects on civilian earnings is inconclusive once mean independence is relaxed.
