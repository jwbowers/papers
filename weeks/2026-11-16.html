Sensitivity Analysis for Linear Estimators

Jacob Dorn, Luther Yap · arxiv · posted 16 November 2026

We propose a framework for building sensitivity analyses for linear estimators. We measure the degree of identification failure by the change of measure between the observed distribution and a hypothetical target distribution under which a standard estimator would identify the parameter of interest. Under L_infinity bounds on that derivative, the identified set has closed-form sharp bounds that require no numerical optimization and that are computable and continuous when the derivative is unbounded, nesting Manski-type worst-case bounds and existing characterizations that require bounded changes of measure. The framework nests several selection models for average potential outcomes, yielding the established worst-case bounds of each design. The framework yields a new model family for regression discontinuity (RD) that delivers the first sensitivity analysis on the conditional average treatment effect under manipulation. We show that a bootstrap procedure yields valid confidence intervals. An empirical application shows that a modest restriction on manipulation delivers bounds far more informative than worst-case bounds.

Interpretable confidence regions for causal discovery

Jian Kang, Arnav Mazumder, Giovanni Carter Lembo, Armeen Taeb · arxiv · posted 16 November 2026

Most work in causal discovery focuses on point estimation of the underlying causal graph, while uncertainty in the learned structure has received substantially less attention. We develop confidence regions for the completed partially directed acyclic graph (CPDAG) identified from observational data. Our approach builds on perturbed PC (Chang et al., 2026), which generates plausible CPDAGs by perturbing the conditional independence tests underlying the widely used PC algorithm. The threshold used in these tests controls how perturbations translate into variation across the resulting CPDAGs. We propose a new thresholding strategy that suppresses spurious variation across runs while preserving asymptotic coverage, producing a substantially tighter collection of plausible CPDAGs. This collection is summarized through confidence balls and model confidence intervals constructed from the partially ordered set of CPDAGs under model inclusion. We also develop new theory and algorithms for the exact computation of these regions. Simulations show that our confidence regions attain good coverage while being substantially smaller and more interpretable than the original perturbed PC collections.

Approximating an Ideal Experiment with Induced Order Statistics

Federico A. Bugni, Ivan A. Canay, Deborah Kim · arxiv · posted 16 November 2026

Many economic questions concern the conditional distribution of an outcome at a fixed covariate value. We refer to observing i.i.d.\ draws from this distribution as an \emph{ideal experiment}, under which many statistical procedures admit exact, finite-sample guarantees. When the covariate is continuously distributed, however, this ideal experiment is unavailable. We provide a general method for approximating this ideal experiment using \emph{induced order statistics}, the outcomes attached to the nearest observed covariates. We derive explicit bounds on the approximation error in Hellinger and total variation distances. These bounds quantify how local approximation errors accumulate as the nearest-neighbor sample grows and provide a unified way to transfer rejection and coverage guarantees across statistical procedures. Under quadratic mean differentiability and local regularity of the covariate distribution, the bounds apply to both interior and boundary conditioning points and give explicit conditions on the growth of the nearest-neighbor sample. We illustrate the approach in four distinct applications: covariate balance testing in regression discontinuity designs, conditional quantile inference, conformal prediction at a fixed covariate value, and policy-irrelevance testing under network interference.

Stratified Randomization Incorporating Historical Control Data

Masatoshi Shoji, Isao Yokota, Carolin Herrmann · arxiv · posted 16 November 2026

Randomized controlled trials are often infeasible in rare diseases because of limited sample size, high costs, or lengthy durations. Hybrid control designs address this by combining a concurrent control arm with historical data. However, existing methods such as propensity score matching (PSM) borrow historical data only at the analysis stage, which limits power gains when covariate distributions differ between historical and concurrent data. We propose a stratified block randomization method for hybrid control trials. Within each stratum, more concurrent subjects are allocated to the treatment arm than to the concurrent control arm, and the difference is filled with historical controls so that the treatment and hybrid control arms are balanced 1:1; once the available historical controls in the stratum are used or a pre-specified overall cap is reached, allocation returns to 1:1. Historical controls are incorporated either by sampling or by weighting. We compared both methods with PSM in terms of type I error rate, power, and sample size via Monte Carlo simulations. The sampling method maintained the type I error rates when there was neither heterogeneity in the outcome model nor unmeasured covariates. It also had power comparable to or greater than that of PSM, particularly when historical controls were unevenly distributed across strata. The power of the weighting method increased with the pooled historical sample size, but its type I error rate was inflated when covariates beyond the stratification factors were present or when stratification factors were dichotomized from continuous covariates. The proposed method incorporates historical data at the allocation stage rather than only at the analysis stage, enabling more efficient use of historical data.

Positive Weights Do Not Certify TWFE Inference: A Design Diagnostic under Treatment-Effect Heterogeneity

Stanis{\l}aw M. S. Halkiewicz · arxiv · posted 16 November 2026

Conventional two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) inference need not be uniformly adequate even when all treatment weights are positive. Under a local treatment-effect drift, the statistic for the equally weighted treated-cell effect has worst-case mean shift $(c/q)\Gamma$, where $\Gamma=\sqrt{N_1}\|w-u\|_2$ is design-only and $q$ is the cluster-score noise scale. Projection matches the exposure to cohort, event-time, additive, and saturated group-time pilots; when residualized treatment is constant within treated group-time cells, $\Gamma_{\mathrm{gt}}=\Gamma$. A direct score estimator is feasible with fixed panel length as clusters grow. Because a debiased quadratic pilot is nonregular at zero, we use two regular one-sided procedures: a reverse-triangle multiplier lower bound to detect unsafe envelopes and covariance-aware noncentral-$\chi^2$ inversion to certify adequacy. At $5\%$ nominal size with a five-point tolerance, the shift threshold is $0.652$. In the Callaway--Sant'Anna minimum-wage panel, where every treated-cell weight is positive, the saturated $95\%$ lower shift bound is $0.793$, so conventional inference is not uniformly certified. A complementary Brazilian property-tax reanalysis with $2{,}840$ municipalities withholds certification in all four classes; its smallest lower bound is $1.040$. Under the maintained many-cluster approximation, castle-doctrine homicide passes for additive cohort-plus-event-time heterogeneity with a $95\%$ upper bound of $0.236$; its saturated covariance lacks one required rank. Divorce is inconclusive. These envelopes are not predictions of realized rejection. The diagnostic separates point correction, uniform exposure, and statistically warranted certification.

Constrained Stochastic Policy Targeting under Heterogeneous Partial Interference

Elena Dal Torrione, Chan Park, Laura Forastiere · arxiv · posted 16 November 2026

Under heterogeneous interference, treating individuals with particular characteristics affects the population average outcome through their heterogeneous direct response to treatment and indirect effect on others, creating opportunities for efficient policy design. For instance, policymakers may minimize resource use by vaccinating individuals identified as superspreaders to achieve a target reduction in disease incidence. In such setting, we first study the problem of learning individualized stochastic treatment policies that minimize population average treatment probability subject to a prespecified outcome target. We propose an estimation approach based on a smooth surrogate for constraint violations and interference-aware inverse-probability-weighting or self-normalized estimators of the population average outcome, and establish finite-sample regret guarantees for both estimators. We also consider the complementary problem of maximizing the population average outcome subject to a constraint on average treatment probability. We apply the proposed methodology to a Colombian conditional cash transfer program aimed at improving school attendance. The application demonstrates that accounting for interference and individualizing treatment probabilities can reduce resource use relative to policies that ignore interference or impose homogeneous treatment probabilities.

Benefits and Costs of Adaptive Sampling

Yu-Shiou Willy Lin, Dae Woong Ham, Iavor Bojinov · arxiv · posted 16 November 2026

Multi-armed bandits are widely used for sequential experimentation in clinical trials, public policy treatment, recommendation systems, and online platforms. While regret minimization and valid inference from adaptively collected data have each been studied individually, one question remains: when does adaptivity \emph{improve estimation precision} relative to uniform designs, and how should inference be balanced against the online cost of experimentation? We first study arm-level mean estimation under mean-squared-error (MSE) objectives. We characterize when an adaptive Neyman allocation, which allocates samples according to arm variance, yields strict MSE improvements over uniform sampling in finite samples. When there is variance heterogeneity across arms, these improvements arise at modest sample sizes, clarifying that adaptivity can be preferable for inference not only asymptotically, but also in finite-sample settings. We then study a joint inference--regret objective that accounts for the cost of assigning units to inferior arms during experimentation. We propose three policies: the Plug-in Oracle Rate Policy, Static-Allocation Rate Policy, and Neyman-Adaptive Rate Policy, all of which attain the oracle-optimal asymptotic rate. The latter two offer computationally efficient and easy-to-implement alternatives by combining existing regret-minimizing algorithms with inference-oriented sampling through closed-form mixing rules, avoiding repeated numerical optimization while being managerially interpretable. We compare their finite-sample statistical performance through simulations and empirically calibrated applications. The results highlight the relative strengths of our policies across instances and their tradeoffs between joint inference--regret performance and computational cost.

Causal and conditional tail coefficients: asymptotics, inference and graph recovery

Axel B\"ucher, Mario Krali · arxiv · posted 16 November 2026

The causal tail coefficient (CTC) measures the influence of an extreme value of one variable on another variable and has been proposed as a tool for causal discovery in heavy-tailed models. Despite its popularity, the asymptotic behavior of its empirical counterpart has not been well understood. We develop an asymptotic theory for the rank-based CTC estimator with an intermediate threshold under nonparametric conditions that are satisfied by all standard copula models. A key observation is that the CTC is determined by the mean of a conditional tail limit distribution, which clarifies its interpretation and determines the asymptotic variance of the estimator. We establish joint asymptotic normality for the entire CTC matrix and prove consistency of a multiplier bootstrap for simultaneous inference. For heavy-tailed linear and recursive max-linear structural equation models, we show that the CTC equals one if and only if the first variable is an ancestor of the second, which leads to confidence graphs for the ancestral graph with asymptotic coverage guarantees. All results extend to general conditional tail coefficients, defined as further functionals of the conditional tail limit distribution. A simulation study and an application to dietary intake data illustrate the methods.

Identification and Inference of Bi-directional Causal Effects in Mendelian Randomization without Instrument Selection

Liyuan Mo, Qingyuan Zhao · arxiv · posted 16 November 2026

Mendelian randomization (MR) uses genetic variants as instruments to estimate causal effects. Bidirectional MR usually uses two reversed unidirectional analyses, but feedback can invalidate instruments in at least one direction and induce correlated pleiotropy. We model both traits as a stable cyclic system and estimate both directional effects from GWAS summary data without instrument selection or a prespecified valid-instrument subset. After LD score normalisation, marginal associations form a heteroscedastic noisy independent component analysis model, assuming the variants' direct effects on the two traits are independent. If those effects are non-Gaussian, this identifies the total-effect matrix up to scaling and permutation; no-self-feedback and stability of the system further resolve these ambiguities. We develop an estimator based on noise-adjusted second- and fourth-order moments, avoiding inversion of the high-dimensional linkage disequilibrium matrix and having computational cost linear in the number of variants. Under weak local dependence, we establish consistency, asymptotic normality, and validity of a chromosome-respecting moving block bootstrap. Simulations show negligible bias, generally reliable interval coverage, and higher rejection rates for nonzero directional effects than focusing-based tests. Three real-data applications illustrate how joint analysis of feedback systems can yield causal decompositions differing from two separate unidirectional analyses.

Regularized parametric modal regression for positive responses with high-dimensional covariates

Christian E. Galarza, V\'ictor H. Lachos, Dashun Liu · arxiv · posted 16 November 2026

Modal regression models the conditional mode rather than the conditional mean, which is attractive for positive and right-skewed responses whose mean is distorted by the upper tail. We propose a regularized version of parametric modal regression for positive responses with high-dimensional covariates, in which the density parameters of the Gamma, Weibull and Lognormal distributions are written as functions of the conditional mode and the log-likelihood is penalized by the Lasso, adaptive Lasso or elastic-net penalties. The estimates are computed by a proximal Newton algorithm, equivalently an iteratively reweighted penalized least-squares scheme, whose inner problem is solved exactly by the coordinate-descent routines of glmnet, and the regularization parameter is selected by the extended Bayesian information criterion. We show that the Lognormal estimator coincides with the Gaussian Lasso applied to the log-transformed response, that under constant dispersion the conditional mean and the conditional mode have the same slopes, and that the estimation error attains the rate $O(\sqrt{s}\lambda_n)$ with no smoothing bias. Simulation studies with up to $p = 1000$ covariates show that the method improves on the Lasso applied to the log-transformed response for Gamma and Weibull responses, and two applications illustrate its use. The method is implemented in the R package ModalNet.

Estimating a Rational Inattention Discrete Choice Model: A Semiparametric Approach

Hanqi Wang · arxiv · posted 16 November 2026

People's choices depend on both the payoffs of the available options and what they learn about those payoffs before deciding. This paper shows how choice data identify both unobserved payoff heterogeneity and the cost of information acquisition in a rational inattention discrete-choice model. I establish conditions for semiparametric point identification using exogenous payoff and information-cost shifters, without imposing parametric forms on the taste-shock distribution or on how the unit cost of information varies with observed characteristics. In the model with binary choice, I construct an unobserved choice threshold whose cumulative distribution function is the choice probability as a function of the payoff shifter. Moments of this threshold identify the payoff coefficients and the unit cost of information at each value of the information-cost shifter. A deconvolution argument then identifies the taste-shock distribution. I then extend identification to the model with multinomial choice. To estimate the model, I propose a sieve minimum-distance estimator based on observed market shares and establish its consistency. For inference, I construct confidence regions by test inversion because binding parameter constraints can make the estimator's limiting distribution nonnormal. In an application to Amazon Mechanical Turk, model counterfactuals show that lower unit costs of information raise the average task-taking probability and reduce wage elasticity.

Randomization protects the average, not the trial: chance covariate imbalance and a calibration of balance $P$ values at the design stage

William J. Dwyer, David Zurakowski · arxiv · posted 17 November 2026

Randomization makes the unadjusted treatment-effect estimate unbiased over the allocations a scheme could produce, although it does not balance any single trial. Chance imbalances on strongly prognostic baseline variables directly threaten the validity and regulatory credibility of trial results, yet what design-stage balance $P$ values imply for bias and error rates is seldom stated. We review evidence on chance covariate imbalance and derive that link under a linear model with one normal prognostic covariate, with simulation checks. Given the realized allocation, the unadjusted estimate is biased by $\beta$ times the difference in covariate means; in standard-error units the bias is $\rho Z_{\mathrm{bal}}$, where $\rho$ is the covariate-outcome correlation and $Z_{\mathrm{bal}}$ the balance z-statistic, so it does not shrink as trials grow. Pre-specified ANCOVA removes this bias, change scores reverse it, and non-collapsibility attenuates odds and hazard ratios even under perfect balance. A worst-balance threshold of $P = 1/e$ marks where the Sellke-Bayarri-Berger bound reaches 1; under complete randomization the worst of $k$ independent balance $P$ values falls below it with probability $1-(1-1/e)^k$, 0.99 for $k=10$. Any balance $P$ value $\geq 0.317$ keeps the two-sided conditional type I error of the unadjusted test at or below 5% for every $\rho$; at $1/e$ the one-sided error in the favoured direction is at most 4.1%, against 13-33% at $P = 0.05$. Design-stage balance scores should be benchmarked against complete randomization, weighted by external prognostic strength, and reported alongside allocation predictability and a pre-specified covariate-adjusted analysis plan.

Generalized Bayes for Causal Inference

Emil Javurek, Dennis Frauen, Yuxin Wang, Stefan Feuerriegel · arxiv · posted 17 November 2026

Uncertainty quantification is central to many applications of causal machine learning, yet principled Bayesian inference for causal effects remains challenging. Standard Bayesian approaches typically require specifying a probabilistic model for the data-generating process, including high-dimensional nuisance components such as propensity scores and outcome regressions. Standard posteriors are thus vulnerable to strong modeling choices, including complex prior elicitation. In this paper, we propose a generalized Bayesian framework for causal inference. Our framework avoids explicit likelihood modeling; instead, we place priors directly on the causal estimands and update these using an identification-driven loss function, which yields generalized posteriors for causal effects. As a result, our framework turns existing loss-based causal estimators into estimators with full uncertainty quantification. Our framework is flexible and applicable to a broad range of causal estimands (e.g., ATE, CATE). Further, our framework can be applied on top of state-of-the-art causal machine learning pipelines (e.g., Neyman-orthogonal meta-learners). For Neyman-orthogonal losses, we show that the generalized posteriors converge to their oracle counterparts and remain robust to first-stage nuisance estimation error. With calibration, we thus obtain valid frequentist uncertainty even when nuisance estimators converge at slower-than-parametric rates. Empirically, we demonstrate that our proposed framework offers causal effect estimation with calibrated uncertainty across several causal inference settings. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first flexible framework for constructing generalized Bayesian posteriors for causal machine learning.

Unit-Level Conformal Inference for Panel Counterfactuals

Ziyi Liu, Yiqing Xu · arxiv · posted 17 November 2026

We develop conformal inference for panel counterfactual estimators using the exchangeability of prediction errors across units rather than over time. Existing conformal methods for panels with few treated units rely on temporal properties of treated-unit residuals, which are vulnerable to strong temporal heterogeneity such as nonstationarity. We instead hold out each control unit, hide its relevant outcomes, and predict them using the same rule applied to the treated unit. Target-date estimators, including difference-in-differences and synthetic control, use controls' pre-treatment and target-date outcomes to construct the counterfactual, allowing both full conformal confidence sets and Jackknife+ intervals. Full-path estimators, including factor-model imputation and matrix completion, also use controls' outcomes at other post-treatment dates; for these, we use the Jackknife+. Both procedures yield finite-sample, period-wise confidence sets under this across-unit exchangeability, without requiring stationarity or random assignment. We characterize when the coverage holds and show why a common prediction rule alone does not ensure it. For multiple treated units, a block Jackknife+ covers their average effect, while a relabeling test is exact for sharp nulls over all treated cells. We show that the synthetic control placebo test is a full conformal test of no effect that extends to full-path estimators. We also relate our procedures to a parametric bootstrap and classify common estimators by the inference they allow. We illustrate our procedures in three empirical applications.

These tests measure conditional independence of outcomes from treatment assignment by asking whether treatment can be predicted from outcomes more accurately than from covariates alone. We design three such tests of conditional independence. First, we propose a conditional randomization test based on an inverse propensity weighted mean squared error test statistic. If the propensity score is known, the test controls size in finite samples. Second, we propose a double-residualization approach, where we partial out covariate effects from both treatment and outcome-based treatment predictions, and test whether the correlation between the residuals is zero. Assessing Distributional Covariate Balance After Weighting: A Practical Guide

Ariel Linden · arxiv · posted 17 November 2026

Health researchers commonly use weighting methods to improve covariate balance when estimating program and intervention effects from observational data. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) and variance ratios assess particular features of balance but may overlook residual differences elsewhere in the covariate distribution. This paper provides practical guidance for using Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS), Anderson-Darling (AD), and Cramer-von Mises (CVM) statistics as complementary distributional balance diagnostics. In an empirical evaluation of a disease management program, weighting based on a main-effects propensity-score model produced SMD and KS statistics below commonly used or proposed balance thresholds of 0.10 for two continuous covariates, which would conventionally be interpreted as indicating acceptable balance. Nevertheless, permutation tests identified residual distributional differences. Adding quadratic terms for these covariates approximately halved their KS statistics, improved variance balance, and eliminated evidence of residual distributional differences, while leaving the estimated program effect substantively unchanged. Supporting simulations showed that KS, AD, and CVM differed in sensitivity to different forms of distributional discrepancy and that the calibration of resampling-based p-values depended on how the reference distribution was constructed. These findings support a practical approach that combines conventional balance measures, graphical assessment, complementary distributional diagnostics, weighting-model refinement, and outcome adjustment when important residual imbalance remains.

PAIR-CI: Calibrated Conditional Independence Testing for Causal Discovery with Incomplete Data

Thomas S. Robinson, Ranjit Lall · arxiv · posted 17 November 2026

The standard constraint-based paradigm for causal discovery with incomplete data -- impute first, test second -- is frequently miscalibrated: any consistent conditional independence (CI) test rejects a true null with probability approaching 1 when imputation error induces spurious conditional dependence. We introduce PAIR-CI, a nonparametric CI test that restores calibration by integrating multiple imputation directly into the inferential procedure via a paired permutation design. PAIR-CI compares cross-validated models that include and exclude the candidate variable while receiving the same imputed conditioning set, so that imputation error cancels in their loss difference rather than contaminating the test statistic. A variance estimator that nests a provably consistent cross-validation estimator inside Rubin's rules yields inference that is asymptotically valid in the many-imputation limit and conservative by a derived, sign-definite mechanism -- to our knowledge, the first formal result combining these two inferential frameworks. In simulations, existing imputation-based CI tests exhibit false positive rates of 28-45% when data are missing not at random (MNAR), whereas PAIR-CI's pooled rate is at most 3.6%, exceeding the nominal 5% only in some cells with five or more conditioning variables. Integrated into the PC algorithm, PAIR-CI is stable across functional forms and missingness mechanisms, whereas every parametric pipeline we consider, including missing-value PC, has a regime in which it fails badly. On nonlinear network benchmarks it reduces structural Hamming distance by 18% (ALARM) and 11% (HAILFINDER) relative to missing-value PC and by 31-48% relative to test-wise deletion and imputation-based Fisher-$Z$ pipelines, while attaining the highest skeleton F1; when relationships are linear, a well-specified parametric pipeline remains preferable.
